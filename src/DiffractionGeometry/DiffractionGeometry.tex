\section{Introduction}\label{\chabbr:sec:introduction}

In this chapter, we will describe the geometry of the diffraction process in a way that ignores the nature of the radiation used for the diffraction experiment. We will see that diffraction has a purely geometric aspect as well as an intensity aspect, and the two can be treated separate from each other.  The primary goal of a diffraction experiment is to determine how atoms or molecules are arranged in 3D.  When we think about a crystal structure, then the primary unknowns are: the lattice parameters $\{a,b,c,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}$, the symmetry (point group and space group), the atom types and positions in terms of fractional coordinates in the unit cell, and, in some cases, the orientation of the lattice with respect to an external reference frame.  Diffraction techniques allow us to determine all of these quantities.  

While the diffraction geometry can be described with respect to the direct crystallographic basis, it turns out that it is much more convenient to work in the reciprocal basis.  This should not be a surprise since waves are described by a wave vector with dimensions of inverse length; the wave vector is normal to the wave front, in the same way that a reciprocal lattice vector is normal to a lattice plane. The interplanar spacing, $d_{hkl}$, for the $(hkl)$ planes is the reciprocal of the length of the vector $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, just as the wavelength is the reciprocal of the length of the wave vector.

We will begin with the derivation of the Laue Equations, followed by the introduction of Bragg's Law in both direct and reciprocal spaces.  Then we describe the concept of the Ewald sphere, a geometric construction to determine the direction of a diffracted wave. In the final sections of this chapter we describe several important experimental diffraction geometries for x-rays, electrons and neutrons.

\section{The Laue Equations}\label{\chabbr:sec:LaueEq}

\begin{floatingfigure}[r]{0.4\textwidth}
\centering
{\includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{\graphicspath Laue.pdf}}
{\caption{\label{\chabbr:fig:1DLaue}\small Illustration of the Laue diffraction condition for a 1D string of atoms.}}
\end{floatingfigure}
\noindent We begin this section with a 1D example derivation of the geometrical condition that must be satisfied to have \indexit{constructive interference} from a line of identical atoms with lattice basis vector $\mathbf{a}$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:1DLaue}. The incident wave travels in the direction given by the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0$ at an angle $\alpha_i$ with respect to the line of atoms.  Each atom will scatter a spherical wave with the same wavelength as the incident wave.  To have constructive interference in the direction $\hat{\mathbf{s}}$, the scattered waves from atoms $B$ and $C$ must be in-phase with each other; this, in turn, requires that the path length difference $AB-CD$ between two waves must be an integer multiple of the wavelength.  We have
\[
	AB-CD = a\cos\alpha_i-a\cos\alpha_o=\mathbf{a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0 - \mathbf{a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}} = n_x\lambda\ ,
\]
where $n_x$ is an integer.  Bringing the wavelength to the other side of the equation and introducing wave vectors we obtain:
\[
	\mathbf{a}\cdot\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0}{\lambda} - \frac{\hat{\mathbf{s}}}{\lambda}\right) = \mathbf{a}\cdot\left(\mathbf{k}_0-\mathbf{k}\right) = n_x\ .
\]
If we set $\Delta\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_0$, then we have $\mathbf{a}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{k}=n_x$ (we have absorbed the minus sign into the integer $n_x$), or, in words, the projection of the scattering vector $\Delta\mathbf{k}$ onto the basis vector $\mathbf{a}$ must be an integer. 

In a 3D lattice, the same type of condition must be true for each of the basis vector directions, so we obtain a set of three equations known as the \indexit{Laue Equations}, first derived by \indexit{Max von Laue} in 1912:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	\mathbf{a}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{k}&=n_x\ ;\\
	\mathbf{b}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{k}&=n_y\ ;\\
	\mathbf{c}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{k}&=n_z\ .
\end{split}\label{\chabbr:eq:LaueEq}
\end{equation}
Note that this is a very restrictive set of equations: the projection of the scattering vector onto \textit{each} of the basis vectors must be an integer.  If we take an integer linear combination of the basis vectors, i.e., a lattice translation vector $\mathbf{t}_{uvw}$, then we have:
\[
	\mathbf{t}_{uvw}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{k} = (u\mathbf{a}+v\mathbf{b}+w\mathbf{c})\cdot\Delta\mathbf{k}=u\,n_x+v\,n_y+w\,n_z = n\ ;
\]
the last equality simply states that the projection of the scattering vector onto \textit{any} lattice translation vector must be an integer.  From our knowledge of crystallography we can easily see that this relation can only be valid if the scattering vector is equal to a reciprocal lattice vector, i.e., $(n_x,n_y,n_z)=(h,k,l)$ and $\Delta\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ for the lattice planes $(hkl)$.  The Laue equations therefore lead to the important result \textit{that the scattering vector must be normal to a lattice plane in order to have constructive interference between waves scattered by the atoms in that plane.}  \\


\section{Bragg's Law in Direct and Reciprocal Space}\label{\chabbr:sec:BraggsLaw}

\subsection{Derivation of Bragg's Law}\label{\chabbr:ssec:BraggDeriv}
From the Laue equations we can immediately obtain \indexit{Bragg's Law} in reciprocal space by writing the equation in terms of the incident and diffracted wave vectors:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\rightarrow \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:BraggR}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}(a) shows the relevant vectors with the incident wave vector  $\mathbf{k}_0$ drawn horizontally.  Note that the scattering vector $\Delta\mathbf{k}$ is perpendicular to the bisector line of the angle between $\mathbf{k}_0$ and $\mathbf{k}$.  If we rotate the whole drawing clockwise by an angle $\theta$ we obtain the configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}(b).  The horizontal line now represents the lattice plane with Miller indices $(hkl)$, and the scattering vector $\Delta\mathbf{k}$, which according to the Laue equation is equal to $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, now is oriented vertically, as it should be.  This configuration is the starting point for the traditional derivation of Bragg's Law in direct space.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\graphicspath Bragg.pdf}
\caption{(a) configuration of wave vectors and scattering vector; (b) same as (a) but rotated clockwise by $\theta$; (c) illustration for the determination of the path length difference between waves scattered from successive lattice planes $(hkl)$.}
\label{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}
\end{figure}

Consider the drawing in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}(c); we consider the lattice planes to be semi-transparent for the incident x-ray beam with wave vector $\mathbf{k}_0$. If the angle between the plane and the incident beam is equal to $\theta$, then Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}(b) tells us that the diffracted beam should also have an angle $\theta$ with respect to the plane.  If the waves labeled $1$ and $2$ are in-phase, then we will have constructive interference if waves $1'$ and $2'$ are also in-phase. This occurs when the path length difference $\Delta$ between the waves is an integer multiple of the wavelength $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta = PO'+O'Q = OO'(\sin\theta+\sin\theta) = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta = n\lambda\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:Bragg1}
\end{equation}
In optical diffraction experiments, for instance diffraction from an optical grating, the integer $n$ describes the order of the diffracted beam.  In crystallography, we typically bring $n$ to the left hand side of the equation; it is easy to see that the following relation is valid:
\[
	\frac{d_{hkl}}{n}=d_{nh\,nk\,nl}\ ,
\]
which means that diffraction from, say, the $(110)$ planes should be considered as being different from diffraction from the $(220)$, $(330)$, $\ldots$ planes.  In other words, we do not consider diffraction orders at all.  Bragg's Law in direct space is then given by:
\begin{equation}
	2d_{hkl}\sin\theta = \lambda\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:Bragg2}
\end{equation}
We can easily derive this relation directly from the Laue equation by projecting $\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_0=\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ onto the unit plane normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$:
\[
	\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{k}-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{k}_0=\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\rightarrow \frac{\sin\theta}{\lambda}+\frac{\sin\theta}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{d_{hkl}}\rightarrow 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta = \lambda\ .
\]
Note that Bragg's Law in direct space is a scalar relation; therefore, we can use this equation to determine the diffraction angle, when the interplanar spacing and wavelength are known, but the equation contains no information about the direction of the incident and diffracted beam.  All we know is that the angle between the two beams must be equal to $2\theta$. The reciprocal version of Bragg's Law, on the other hand, is a relation between vectors so that we do have information about the actual direction of the diffracted beam.  This is also the reason why the geometry of the diffraction process is almost always described in reciprocal space.

\begin{floatingfigure}[r]{0.5\textwidth}
\centering
{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{\graphicspath Kosselcones.pdf}}
{\caption{\label{\chabbr:fig:Kosselcones}\small Illustration of the 3D nature of the diffraction condition; blue surfaces are Kossel cones.}}
\end{floatingfigure}
\noindent The drawing in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}(c) represents the traditional way to illustrate Bragg's Law, but it is somewhat misleading because it leaves out part of the story.  Bragg's Law in reciprocal space states that the three vectors, $\mathbf{k}_0$, $\mathbf{k}$, and $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ are coplanar vectors; all three vectors lie in the plane of the drawing. What is missing in this drawing is the 3D nature of the diffraction process. The vectors have to be coplanar, but there are an infinite number of planes for which this condition is satisfied; all of the planes that contain the vector $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ are potential planes for which Bragg's Law can be satisfied.  This is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Kosselcones}. The figure shows two conical surfaces (blue), one on each side of the  $(hkl)$ plane (green); the cones have an opening angle at the apex of $\pi-2\theta$. The incident wave vector $\mathbf{k}_0$ can lie anywhere on the top or bottom surface of the cone, pointing towards the apex; $\mathbf{k}_0$ and $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ form a plane normal to the lattice plane $(hkl)$ (orange) and the diffracted wave vector must lie along the intersection of this plane and the cone surface on the side opposite of $\mathbf{k}_0$.  The diffraction condition, i.e., Bragg's Law in reciprocal space, is thus satisfied for all incident beam directions that lie on the cone and go through the apex.  The two cones are commonly known as \indexit{Kossel cones}.

\subsection{Bragg's Law and momentum transfer}\label{\chabbr:ssec:Braggmomentum}
The \indexit{de Broglie equation} states that the momentum of a particle is inversely proportional to the wavelength, with the proportionality constant equal to \indexit{Planck's constant}, $h$, which, in SI units, is defined as exactly $6.62607\times 10^{-34}$ Js.  In vector form, the relation is given by:
\[
	\mathbf{p} = h\,\mathbf{k}\ . 
\]
The Laue equation derived above states that diffraction from the $(hkl)$ planes requires that the scattering vector, $\Delta\mathbf{k}$, be equal to the reciprocal lattice vector $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$. Combining the two equations we find that the \indexit{momentum transfer} is given by:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta\mathbf{p} = h\,\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:momentumtransfer}
\end{equation}
This equation states that the allowed momentum transfers for elastic scattering (i.e., scattering without energy transfer) are given by the reciprocal lattice vectors (apart from the scaling factor $h$).  This allows us to interpret the reciprocal lattice from a new point of view: \textit{the reciprocal lattice represents the set of all allowed elastic momentum transfer vectors.} Stated differently: \textit{momentum space and reciprocal space are the same, apart from the scale factor $h$}.

It is important to note that this relation does not specify the type of particle or radiation used for the diffraction process. If we know the lattice parameters of the crystal structure, then we can compute the lengths of all the allowed momentum transfer vectors $\Delta\mathbf{p}_{hkl}$ (note that we replace $h$ by $2\pi\hbar$ to avoid confusion with the $h$ Miller index):
\begin{equation}
	\vert\Delta\mathbf{p}_{hkl}\vert = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{d_{hkl}}=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{a}\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:momentumtransfer2}
\end{equation}
where the second equality is valid for cubic crystal structures.  As an example, consider the $(220)$ planes in Al with lattice parameter $a=0.4049$ nm.  Plugging the numbers in to the equation we find for the momentum transfer for elastic scattering from these planes:
\[
	\vert\Delta\mathbf{p}_{220}\vert = \frac{6.62607\times 10^{-34}}{0.4049\times 10^{-9}}\sqrt{8} = 4.62863\times 10^{-24}\text{ kg\,m/s}\ .
\]
If we consider two x-ray photon beams, one for Cu K$\alpha$ with $\lambda=0.15418$ nm, the other for Mo K$\alpha$ with $\lambda=0.07107$ nm, and an electron beam with energy $30$ keV or wavelength $\lambda=0.00708$ nm, then we can compare the diffraction condition for the $(220)$ planes of Al for all three cases. The momentum transfer is the same for all three incident beams because elastic momentum transfer is determined \textit{only} by the interplanar spacing $d_{220}$.  Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:diffcomparison} shows the three sets of incoming and diffracted wave vectors at the correct relative scale. 

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\graphicspath diffcomparison.pdf}
\caption{To-scale comparison of the diffraction condition for the $(220)$ planes of Al for Cu K$\alpha$ and Mo K$\alpha$ radiation as well as $30$ keV electrons. }
\label{\chabbr:fig:diffcomparison}
\end{figure}

Note that this drawing also shows a significant difference between the magnitudes of the Bragg angles; when we solve the Bragg equation for $\theta$, we have:
\[
	\theta = \text{arcsin}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2d_{hkl}}\right)\ ;
\]
for the cases specified above we have Bragg angles of $34.58^{\circ}$ for Cu K$\alpha$ radiation, $14.37^{\circ}$ for Mo K$\alpha$, and $1.42^{\circ}$ for $30$ keV electrons. For $200$ keV electrons, which is a typical value for a transmission electron microscope, the wavelength is $2.508$ pm, leading to a Bragg angle of $0.502^{\circ}$ for the Al $(220)$ planes. For such small angles it is common practice to express them in milli-radians (mrad) instead of degrees; we have $\theta=8.76$ mrad.

\subsection{The Bragg equation using unit vectors only}\label{\chabbr:ssec:unitvectors}
For some diffraction applications it turns out to be useful to have a form of Bragg's Law that only contains unit vectors. We start from Bragg's Law in reciprocal space:
\[
	\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g} \rightarrow \frac{\hat{\mathbf{s}}}{\lambda}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0}{\lambda}+\mathbf{g}\rightarrow \hat{\mathbf{s}}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0+\lambda\mathbf{g}\ .
\]
We can replace the wavelength by the left-hand side of the direct space Bragg equation, and use $d\mathbf{g}=\hat{\mathbf{g}}$: 
\[
	\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g} \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf{s}}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0+2d\sin\theta\,\mathbf{g}\rightarrow \hat{\mathbf{s}}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0+2\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}\ .
\]
From Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Bragg}(b) we find that $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{g}}=\cos(\theta+\pi/2)$, and this is a negative number since the two vectors enclose an angle larger than $\pi/2$; therefore we have:
\[
	\theta= \text{arccos}\left(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{g}}\right)-\frac{\pi}{2}\rightarrow \sin\theta = -\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{g}}\ ;
\]
the final unit vector form of Bragg's equation then reads:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g} \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf{s}}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0-2(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{g}})\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:Braggunitvectors}
\end{equation}
The fact that the wavelength is absent in this equation provides convincing evidence that the diffraction condition is a purely geometrical condition that only depends on the relative orientation of the vectors $ \hat{\mathbf{s}}_0$ and $\hat{\mathbf{g}}$.  Note that the unit vector expression is only valid when the diffraction condition is satisfied.\\

\section{The Ewald Sphere Construction}\label{\chabbr:sec:Ewald}
When we consider Bragg's Law in reciprocal space, we see that the three vectors, $\mathbf{k}_0$, $\mathbf{k}$, and $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ can be drawn as a triangle, with the wave vectors starting in a common point that we will label $C$. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Ewald1}(a), the origin of reciprocal space, which is the starting point of all vectors $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, is labeled $O$, and the point where $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ meet must be a reciprocal lattice point.  Since diffraction is an \indexit{elastic scattering process}, i.e., there is no energy exchange so the wavelength remains constant, the wave vectors have equal length; this means that the points $G$ and $O$ lie at the same distance from $C$. This, in turn, means that $G$ and $O$ lie on a circle with center at $C$ and radius $1/\lambda$; this circle is known as the \indexit{Ewald circle} in 2D, and the \indexit{Ewald sphere} in 3D (Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Ewald1}(b)). We find that \textit{the diffraction condition is satisfied for every reciprocal lattice point that lies on the Ewald sphere}.


\subsection{The Ewald sphere equation}\label{\chabbr:ssec:Ewaldeq}
In 3D, the equation of a sphere of radius $R$ and center at $\mathbf{r}_0$ is given, in vector form, by:
\[
	(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=R^2\ .
\]
Working in reciprocal space coordinates, we have (from Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Ewald}(a)) $\mathbf{r}_0=-\mathbf{k}_0$ and $R=1/\lambda$. Setting $\mathbf{q}$ as an arbitrary vector, then this vector will end on the sphere if:
\[
	(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{k}_0)\cdot(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{k}_0)=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\ .
\]
Working out the dot products and noting that $\mathbf{k}_0\cdot\mathbf{k}_0=1/\lambda^2$ we find:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{q}+2\mathbf{k}_0)=0\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:eqEwald}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Ewald2}(a) illustrates this relation; if a reciprocal space vector $\mathbf{q}$ lies on the Ewald sphere, then that vector is orthogonal to $\mathbf{q}+2\mathbf{k}_0$. This is a general relation that could actually be used to define a circle (or a sphere) as the locus or points that satisfy equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:eqEwald}).  For points $\mathbf{q}$ that lie inside the Ewald sphere, the angle $\omega$ is larger than $\pi/2$, so that the dot product becomes negative; for points outside the sphere, the dot product is positive.

If the diffraction condition is satisfied, then we have $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ which leads to:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\cdot(\mathbf{g}_{hkl}+2\mathbf{k}_0) = \mathbf{g}_{hkl}\cdot(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}_0) = 0\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:eqEwald2}
\end{equation}
We have used Bragg's Law to go from the first expression to the second.  This relation is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Ewald2}(b); the vector $\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}_0$ lies on the perpendicular bisector plane of $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ in exact Bragg condition.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\graphicspath Ewald2.pdf}
\caption{(a) Illustration of the relation $\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{q}+2\mathbf{k}_0)=0$; (b) shows that the point $\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{k}$ lies on the perpendicular bisector plane of $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$.}
\label{\chabbr:fig:Ewald2}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The Ewald sphere for x-rays, neutrons, and electrons}\label{\chabbr:ssec:Ewaldxne}
We have defined the Ewald sphere by positioning the incident wave vector $\mathbf{k}_0$ so that it ends in the origin of reciprocal space, $O$, and drawing a sphere of radius $1/\lambda$ with center $C$ at the start of $\mathbf{k}_0$. From the diffraction condition drawings in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:diffcomparison} we immediately note that the Ewald sphere for electrons will generally be much larger than that for x-ray photons and neutrons, which have wavelengths in a similar range.  In this section, we will consider more than one reciprocal lattice point and analyze how that affects diffraction processes.

The fact that a reciprocal lattice point must fall on the Ewald sphere dictates what we must do to perform diffraction measurements; if we start with a randomly oriented sample (let's consider a single crystal for now), and a randomly oriented incident beam $\mathbf{k}_0$, then it is immediately clear that a reciprocal lattice point will only accidentally fall on the Ewald sphere and give rise to a diffracted beam. Furthermore, it is unlikely that the x-ray detector we employ will be ``looking'' in the right direction to detect this beam. This means that we must \textit{design} our diffractometer such that we maximize the probability of detecting a diffracted beam.  Fortunately, the Laue equation (or Bragg's Law in reciprocal space) tells us exactly what geometrical condition we need to satisfy; as a consequence, \textit{all} diffraction instruments are designed to implement the geometry of Bragg's Law.

In general, only reciprocal lattice points that lie at most a distance $2/\lambda$ from the origin can give rise to a diffracted beam; since
\[
	\theta = \text{arcsin}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2d_{hkl}}\right)\ ,
\]
the argument of the arcsin function must be at the most equal to $1$, so we must have
\[
	\frac{1}{d_{hkl}} < \frac{2}{\lambda}\ ,
\]
which means that \textit{only lattice planes with an interplanar spacing larger than half the wavelength can give rise to a diffracted beam}.

We have two ways to influence the probability that a reciprocal lattice point will fall on the Ewald sphere: (1) we can move the incident beam orientation relative to the stationary sample, or (2) we can rotate the sample with respect to a stationary beam.  Since the reciprocal lattice is rigidly attached to the crystal lattice, eventually every reciprocal lattice point within a distance $2/\lambda$ will pass through the Ewald sphere surface twice when either the sample or the incident beam are swept through all possible orientations..  Both approaches are used in commercial instruments, and we will highlight several geometries and techniques in section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:DiffractionGeometries}. For now we will only consider some general observations about diffraction geometry.

Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Espheres} shows a schematic simple cubic reciprocal lattice for a crystal structure with lattice parameter $0.4$ nm; two Ewald spheres are superimposed on this drawing, one for Cu K$\alpha$ x-rays (blue sphere), the other for $30$ keV electrons (green sphere). For both spheres, the incident beam direction is parallel to the $[001]$ direction. The Cu K$\alpha$ Ewald sphere has a radius of $6.486$ nm$^{-1}$ whereas the electron Ewald sphere has a radius of $143.287$ nm$^{-1}$ or about $22$ times larger than the x-ray Ewald sphere. $30$ keV electrons are typically used in a scanning electron microscope, whereas the energy is significantly higher for a transmission electron microscope; $200$ or $300$ keV electrons are the norm, which leads to Ewald spheres with radius $398.72$ nm$^{-1}$ and $507.87$ nm$^{-1}$ respectively.  This makes it clear that the number of reciprocal lattice points that can potentially lie on the Ewald sphere is orders of magnitude larger for electron diffraction than for x-ray and neutron diffraction.

\section{Relaxation of the Diffraction Condition}\label{\chabbr:sec:relaxedBragg}
When we take the Ewald sphere interpretation of the Bragg equation, $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, at face value, we observe that there is a bit of a problem.  The Ewald sphere construction involves taking a reciprocal lattice point, which is an infinitesimal point, and placing it on the sphere, which is an infinitesimal shell; the probability of this happening is essentially zero, so from a purely mathematical point of view, diffraction should be impossible.  There are two reasons why we need to relax the strict Bragg equation so that it applies to real diffraction processes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{wavelength distribution}: when we generate a monochromatic beam of incident particles or waves, there is always some spread of the particle energy or wavelength.  For instance, for Cu K$\alpha$ radiation, the photon energy profile has a full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) of about $2.3$ eV \cite{mendenhall2017a} or a wavelength spread of $0.044$ pm. This effectively means that the Ewald sphere actually has a finite thickness corresponding to the wavelength distribution; this is true for all types of incident radiation.  Thus, the Ewald sphere is not an infinitesimally thin shell.
\item \textit{finite sample size}: when a crystal structure is described by a crystallographic space group, the implicit assumption is that the crystal is infinitely large because the space group is an infinite group (translation operations will produce unit cells at arbitrarily large distances from the origin). This leads to infinitesimal reciprocal lattice points, i.e., an infinite volume in direct space has the inverse volume in reciprocal space, which is zero volume.  In reality, space group symmetry is always broken by the sample surfaces (real samples are always of finite size), so that the reciprocal lattice points actually have a shape and a volume (see also section~\ref{FOURIE:ssec:3DCFT} in the Foundational Chapter on Fourier Series and Transforms).
\end{enumerate}
We conclude that, for diffraction to occur, there must be an overlap between the finite reciprocal lattice point and the thin but finite thickness Ewald shell.  This can be described mathematically by modifying the Bragg equation:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\rightarrow \mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g}_{hkl}+\mathbf{s}\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:relaxedBragg}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{s}$ is a (short) vector in reciprocal space, known as the \indexit{excitation error} or \indexit{deviation parameter}, that is a measure for how far removed the reciprocal lattice point is from the Ewald sphere; the precise definition of $\mathbf{s}$ depends on the experimental and sample geometry.

A more detailed mathematical analysis of the effect of sample shape on the reciprocal lattice can be found in section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:sampleshape} at the end of this chapter.  By way of example, let us consider a spherical grain with radius $R$.  A derivation in section~\ref{FOURIE:ssec:3DCFT} shows that the \indexit{shape amplitude} of a sphere is given by the following expression which only depends on the radial distance $k$:
\[
	D(k) = 3V\frac{j_1(k\,R)}{k\,R}\ ,
\]
where $j_1(x)$ is a spherical Bessel function of order $1$ and $V$ is the volume of the sphere.  The shape of the reciprocal lattice point for a diffracting volume of spherical shape is then given by the square of this function (in general the modulus-squared) which is:
\[
	I(k) = 9V^2 \left(\frac{j_1(k\,R)}{k\,R}\right)^2\ .
\]

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\graphicspath EwaldFiniteSample.pdf}
\caption{(a) Illustration of a reciprocal lattice (small red sphere) satisfying the Bragg condition $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_0+\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$; the grain has a spherical shape, leading to the shape intensity radial profile shown in (c).  (b) shows that the shape intensity still intersects the Ewald sphere, even when the reciprocal lattice point is slightly outside the sphere.}
\label{\chabbr:fig:EwaldFiniteSample}
\end{figure}

This function is shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:EwaldFiniteSample}(c) for a unit radius sphere and has spherical symmetry since it only depends of the radial distance  $k$. If the Bragg condition is satisfied, then we know that $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ lies on the Ewald sphere for some $(hkl)$, which is shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:EwaldFiniteSample}(a); the fuzzy reddish halo surrounding the small red sphere represents the shape intensity, i.e., the reciprocal lattice point is spread out over a spherical volume and the shape intensity decreases radially away from that point.  If the reciprocal lattice point does not quite lie on the sphere, as shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:EwaldFiniteSample}(b), then the halo can still intersect the Ewald sphere, and there may be some diffracted intensity. As the point moves through the Ewald sphere, the diffracted intensity will smoothly increase with a profile given by the expression above, reach a maximum when the point is precisely on the Ewald sphere, and then decrease again.  The precise 3D shape of the intensity halo depends on the shape of the diffracting volume.

\subsection{Debye-Scherrer Diffractometer}\label{\chabbr:ssec:DebyeScherrer}
If we take a powder sample or a polycrystalline sample, and we assume that the orientations of the grains are fully random, then the probability of a particular reciprocal lattice vector, $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, pointing in any given direction is a constant; the locus of all the endpoints of the vector $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ is then a sphere with radius $\vert\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\vert=1/d_{hkl}$. If we consider diffraction from those planes, then the diffraction condition will be satisfied along the intersection of this sphere with the Ewald sphere. This is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:powder}(a); the Ewald sphere is shown in blue for Cu K$\alpha$ radiation, and the sample is represented by a thin blue cylinder going through the origin. The incident wave vector $\mathbf{g}_0$ is shown in red. The intersection of the Ewald sphere with the $(111)$ sphere is a circle (shown in black) normal to the wave vector.  The location $\kappa$ along the red axis and the radius $\rho$ of this intersection circle are readily shown to be given by:
\[
	\kappa = \frac{2\sin^2\theta}{\lambda} \quad\text{ and }\quad \rho= \frac{1}{d_{hkl}}\left[1-\left(\frac{\lambda}{2d_{hkl}}\right)^2 \right]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\cos\theta}{d_{hkl}}\ ,
\]
where we used Bragg's Law in the final equation.  For the $(111)$ planes of Al, with lattice parameter $a=0.4049$ nm, the scattering angle is given by $2\theta_{111}=38.51^{\circ}$. For the $(200)$ planes, we have $2\theta=44.76^{\circ}$ and the second diffraction cone is shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:powder}(b).  We conclude that, for a polycrystalline sample with randomly oriented grains, the diffracted beams lie on the surfaces of concentric cones centered around the incident beam direction.  For scattering angles larger than $90^{\circ}$, the cones will be on the opposite  side of the point $C$.

\begin{figure}[t!]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{\graphicspath DebyeScherrer.pdf}
\caption{(a) Schematic cross-section drawing of a Debye-Scherrer diffraction camera. (b) simulated Debye-Scherrer pattern for Al and Cu K$\alpha$ radiation; the area inside the red rectangle represents a typical photographic film size with aspect ratio around $5:1$. }
\label{\chabbr:fig:DebyeScherrer}
\end{figure}

Now that we have an understanding of the diffraction geometry for a polycrystalline sample with random grain orientations, let us take a look at one of the earliest powder diffractometers, the \indexit{Debye-Scherrer camera}, developed around 1916. There are many variations on the Debye-Scherrer camera, among others the \indexit{Seeman-Bohlin camera} and the \indexit{Guinier camera}. Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:DebyeScherrer} shows a schematic cross-section of such a camera. It consists of a flat cylindrical enclosure with an x-ray collimator on one side and a beam stop on the other.  The sample is typically a thin wire (shown in blue at the center of the camera) or a glass capillary filled with powder. The incident x-ray beam generates diffraction cones as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:powder}, but the cones are truncated by the walls of the enclosure. Around the vertical wall, a photographic strip is mounted going all the way around, with circular cutouts for the collimator and the beam stop. The diffraction cones intersect the film along approximately circular arc segments as shown schematically in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:DebyeScherrer}(b).  To determine the scattering angle $2\theta$ for a particular diffraction cone, we note that the angular distance between the centers of the holes in the film ($A$ corresponds to the position of the collimator, $B$ to the beam stop) is equal to $180^{\circ}$; measuring the position of the arc where it intersects the center line of the film and dividing by $L$ then immediately results in the value of $2\theta$:
\begin{equation}
	2\theta = 180^{\circ} \frac{X}{L}\ .
\end{equation}
Note that the center line of the film is the only location where there are no projection distortions; Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:DebyeScherrer}(b) is a simulated pattern covering a much larger vertical range than normally used in these cameras. The red rectangle represents the film aspect ratio that is typically placed inside the cylindrical camera. The simulation was carried out for fcc Al using Cu K$\alpha$ radiation. Note that the deviations from a circle shape become substantial outside of the red rectangle.

\subsection{Laue Diffraction Patterns}\label{\chabbr:ssec:LauePatterns}
The diffraction geometries described thus far, Bragg-Brentano and Debye-Scherrer, are ideally suited for the study of polycrystalline or powder samples.  The diffracted intensity lies on concentric conical surfaces and is readily intercepted with a photographic film or a planar digital detector in modern setups. In this section, we consider a single crystal sample (or polycrystalline with a large grain size); for such a sample, there are discrete diffracted beams that fall on the same diffraction cones as shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:powder}, but in general, very few reciprocal lattice points will fall on the Ewald sphere.  In a \indexit{Laue diffractometer}, the probability of detecting a diffracted beam is increased by removing the filter that eliminates the K$\beta$ peak and allowing a range of wavelengths to simultaneously scatter from the sample.  The voltage of the x-ray tube sets the smallest wavelength, $\lambda_{\text{min}}$, or the largest wave number, $k_{0,\text{max}}$.  We have seen in section~\ref{\chabbr:ssec:Ewaldxne} that a diffracted beam can only occur for planes with an interplanar spacing larger than half the wavelength. The scalar version of Bragg's Law also tells us that there is a largest wavelength beyond which no diffraction will occur. If we consider the reciprocal lattice and the Ewald sphere construction, then it is easy to see that not a single reciprocal lattice point can fall on the Ewald sphere when the diameter of the sphere is smaller than the length of the shortest reciprocal lattice vector, or:
\[
	\frac{2}{\lambda} < \vert\mathbf{g}_{hkl}\vert\quad\text{for all $(hkl)$}\ .
\]
if we represent by $d_{\text{max}}$ the largest interplanar spacing for a given crystal structure, then there is a maximum wavelength, $\lambda_{\text{max}}=2d_{\text{max}}$ above which there are no diffracted beams.  Therefore, for an unfiltered x-ray tube, there is a continuous range of wavelengths $[\lambda_{\text{min}},\lambda_{\text{max}}]$, for which diffracted beams will occur. This, in turn, means that there are two bounding Ewald spheres, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:LaueEwald}(a); all the reciprocal lattice points that lie in between the two spheres can simultaneously give rise to a diffracted beam.  

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\graphicspath LaueEwald.pdf}
\caption{(a) 3D rendering of the two Ewald spheres and reciprocal lattice points that lie between the spheres; (b) construction of the diffracted wave vector $\mathbf{k}_{hkl}$ for an arbitrary point $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ in between the Ewald spheres.}
\label{\chabbr:fig:LaueEwald}
\end{figure}

To determine the direction of a diffracted beam for an arbitrary reciprocal lattice point in between the two spheres we need to draw the reciprocal lattice vector, $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:LaueEwald}(b); we learned in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:Ewald2}(b) that the center of the Ewald sphere must lie on the perpendicular bisector of $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, which is drawn as a dashed black line.  Where this line intersects $\mathbf{k}_{\text{max}}$ (orange circle) we find the center, $C_{hkl}$, of the Ewald sphere that goes through the point $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$; connecting $C_{hkl}$ with the reciprocal lattice point then results in the direction of the diffracted wave vector $\mathbf{k}_{hkl}$.  Note that every reciprocal lattice point lies on its own Ewald sphere (dashed green circle) corresponding to a wavelength in the interval $[\lambda_{\text{min}},\lambda_{\text{max}}]$. 

Experimentally, two different geometries are used to intercept the diffracted beams: in \indexit{transmission Laue diffraction} one places a planar detector perpendicular to the incident beam direction at some calibrated distance from the sample (typically several cm for a laboratory setup, but potentially much longer for experiments at a synchrotron source). This configuration requires a thin sample (a foil shape) so that absorption is minimized.  Alternatively, for a bulk sample we can use the \indexit{reflection Laue diffraction} geometry in which the planar detector is placed, again perpendicular to the incident beam, but now on the opposite side of the sample so that reflections with large scattering angles $2\theta$ are intercepted. 

Laue diffraction represents an ideal opportunity to use the unit vector form of Bragg's Law in equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:Braggunitvectors}):
\[
	\hat{\mathbf{s}} = \hat{\mathbf{s}}_0-2(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{g}})\hat{\mathbf{g}}\ .
\]
Since every reciprocal lattice point between the two Ewald spheres has its own wavelength, it makes sense to use a form of the diffraction condition that does not contain the wavelength.  We take the incident beam to travel along the positive $x$ direction; the detector is planar in the $y$--$z$ plane, with the $z$ axis vertical. The incident beam unit vector is then given by $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0=(1,0,0)$, and we have for the direction cosines of $\hat{\mathbf{g}}$ the components $(g_x,g_y,g_z)$.  Substitution into the above equation leads to:
\[
	\hat{\mathbf{s}} = (1,0,0)-2g_x(g_x,g_y,g_z) = (1-2g_x^2,-2g_xg_y,-2g_xg_z)\ .
\]

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\graphicspath LaueDiffraction.pdf}
\caption{3D rendering of the Ewald spheres for transmission and reflection Laue diffraction.}
\label{\chabbr:fig:LaueDiffraction}
\end{figure}

It is readily verified that this is a unit vector since since $g_x^2+g_y^2+g_z^2=1$. The detector screen is at a distance $L$ from the origin and normal to the $x$-axis, so that the $y$ and $z$ axes lie in the plane of the detector. Since $\hat{\mathbf{s}}$ lies in the direction of the diffracted beam, we can determine where on the detector this beam will hit by scaling the unit vector so that its $x$ component becomes equal to $L$; this is accomplished by multiplying the components by $L/(1-2g_x^2)$:
\[
	(y,z) = \pm\frac{2Lg_x}{2g_x^2-1}(g_y,g_z)\ ,
\]
where the $+$ sign is used for the transmission Laue case and the $-$ sign for reflection Laue. From the spot position $(y,z)$ and knowledge of the sample-screen distance $L$ one can invert this relation to obtain the direction cosines of the plane normal; setting $p=\sqrt{L^2+y^2+z^2}$ we have:
\[
	(g_x,g_y,g_z)=\left(-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{L}{2p}}, \frac{y}{\sqrt{2p(p-L)}}, \frac{z}{\sqrt{2p(p-L)}}\right)\ .
\]

Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:LaueDiffraction} shows both a transmission and a reflection Laue pattern for Al, for a maximum x-ray tube voltage of $30$ kV, i.e., $\lambda_{\text{min}}= 0.04135$ nm, and $\lambda_{\text{max}}=0.08$ nm.  The limiting Ewald spheres are shown at the center, with the incident beam (orange vector) along the positive $x$-axis. Only reciprocal lattice points that give rise to a beam that actually intersects either one of the detectors are indicated inside the Ewald volume.  A few observations can be made:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the transmission diffraction pattern contains fewer diffraction spots than the reflection pattern; this is caused by the fact that there is less space between the two Ewald spheres near the origin $O$ than on the opposite side of the larger sphere;
	\item reflections in the transmission pattern tend to line up along ellipses, whereas the reflection pattern shows hyperbolae;
	\item if either detector is moved further away from $O$, then the pattern will be magnified because the distance $L$ occurs linearly in the expression for the coordinates;
	\item it is impossible to determine the wavelength that gave rise to a particular diffraction spot; hence, Laue patterns cannot be used to determine lattice parameters.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\graphicspath LaueBeryl.pdf}
\caption{Simulated Laue transmission pattern of a $[00.1]$-oriented Beryl crystal (left); on the right, the crystal was rotated $10^{\circ}$ around the vertical axis.}
\label{\chabbr:fig:LaueBeryl}
\end{figure}

One of the primary uses of a Laue diffraction pattern is the determination of the orientation of a single crystal. When the incident beam is parallel to a symmetry axis of the crystal, then the Laue pattern will also show that symmetry. An example is shown in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:LaueBeryl} for the hexagonal structure of the mineral Beryl (Be$_3$Al$_2$(SiO$_3$)$_6$); the pattern on the left has the $[00.1]$ axis at the center of the pattern and clearly shows six-fold symmetry and mirror planes, whereas the pattern on the right is rotated $10^{\circ}$  around the vertical axis.  



\section{Example Diffraction Geometry Problems}
In this section we present a few problems related to diffraction geometry, and we work out detailed solutions.

\begin{example}
\textsf{\small Aluminum has a room temperature linear thermal expansion coefficient of $\alpha=23$ $\mu$m/m/K.  If the linear
	thermal expansion coefficient is defined as:
	\[
		\alpha = \frac{1}{L}\frac{\Delta L}{\Delta T},
	\]
	where $L$ is a sample length, then derive the change of the $(220)$ scattering angle $2\theta$ if the Al sample is heated from room temperature to $800$ K.	
	You may assume that $\alpha$ does not change with temperature, and that Cu $K_{\alpha}$ radiation is used.}\\

\noindent\textbf{Solution:} The $d$-spacing for the $(220)$ planes changes with temperature according to :
\[
	\Delta d_{220} = (\alpha\Delta T) d_{220}
\]
so that (taking room temperature to be $20^{\circ}$C):
\[
	d_{220}(800\thinspace K) = d_{220}(293\thinspace  K) \left(1+\alpha\Delta T\right)
\]
With $\Delta T = 507$ K, we have 
\[
	\alpha\Delta T = (23\times 10^{-6})\times 507 = 0.0117
\]
so that (using $a=0.405$ nm):
\[
	d_{220}(800\thinspace K) = \frac{a}{\sqrt{8}}\times 1.0117 = 0.14486\thinspace nm
\]
compared to the room temperature value of $d_{220}(293\thinspace K) =0.14319$ nm.
The position of the room temperature diffracted beam for Cu K$\alpha$ radiation is 
\[
	2\theta_{293\thinspace K}= 2\text{arcsin}\frac{\lambda}{2d_{220}} = 2\text{arcsin}\frac{0.154183}{2\times 0.14319} =  65.148^{\circ};
\]
at $800$ K we have 
\[
	2\theta_{800\thinspace K}=  2\text{arcsin}\frac{0.154183}{2\times 0.14486} =  64.306^{\circ}.
\]
This shift of nearly a full degree is readily measurable. 
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{\chabbr:exa:Laue}
\textsf{\small Consider a primitive cubic structure with lattice parameter $a=0.3345$ nm.  The following questions refer to a Laue diffraction experiment.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Make a scale drawing of the $\mathbf{a}^{\ast} - \mathbf{b}^{\ast}$ plane using $1.0$ nm$^{-1} = 0.5$ cm. [no need to label the reciprocal lattice points]
\item Superimpose on this drawing the Ewald spheres for radiation with a wave length range from $\lambda_1=0.1$ nm to $\lambda_2=0.2$ nm and an incident beam direction $[100]$.  Make sure that the reciprocal lattice points drawn in part (a) go all the way past the largest Ewald sphere.
\item Bragg's law states that a diffracted beam will occur when the reciprocal lattice point falls on the Ewald sphere.  In this case, there is a range of wave lengths and therefore a continuous range of Ewald spheres.   Highlight all the reciprocal lattice points that will give rise to a diffracted beam; note that each reciprocal lattice point will have a different incident wave vector depending on where the point lies between the extremal Ewald spheres.

\begin{example}\label{\chabbr:exa:Laue2}
\textsf{\small A transmission Laue pattern is made of a cubic crystal having a lattice parameter of $0.3$ nm.  The x-ray
beam is horizontal.  The $[0\bar{1}0]$ axis of the crystal points along the beam towards the x-ray source,
and the $[001]$ axis points vertically upward.  The planar detector is $6$ cm from the center of the sample and normal to the $[010]$ axis.  It will be useful to make a simple 2D sketch of this geometry, and to also draw the $(0\bar{2}4)$ plane.
\begin{enumerate}
\item What is the wavelength $\lambda$ (in nm) of the radiation diffracted from the $(0\bar{2}4)$ planes ?
\item Where (i.e., how high above the horizontal plane, in cm) will the $0\bar{2}4$ reflection strike the detector?
\end{enumerate}}

\noindent\textbf{Solutions:} 		
\begin{enumerate}
\item The interplanar spacing $d_{0\bar{2}4}$ is given by $d_{0\bar{2}4}=\frac{a}{\sqrt{20}}$.
From Bragg's law we know that~:
\[
	2d\sin\theta=\frac{2a}{\sqrt{20}}\sin\theta=\lambda
\]
The angle $\theta$ is $90^{\circ}$ minus the angle between the $(0\bar{2}4)$ plane normal 
and the $[0\bar{1}0]$ direction, see Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:example4}.  We find~:
\[
	\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta)=\sin\theta=\frac{[0\bar{2}4].[0\bar{1}0]}{\sqrt{20}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{20}}
\]
and hence 
\[
	\lambda=\frac{2a\times 2}{20}=\frac{a}{5}=0.0600\mbox{ nm}
\]

\section{Mathematical Analysis of the Effect of Sample Shape}\label{\chabbr:sec:sampleshape}
In this section we provide a more in-depth analysis of the sample shape and how it affects the shape of the reciprocal lattice points; the necessary mathematical concepts can be found in the Foundational Chapter on Fourier Series and Transforms.  

We begin with the direct space lattice, which is one of the Bravais lattices.  Each lattice node can be reached by a translation vector $\mathbf{t}_{uvw}=u\mathbf{a}+v\mathbf{b}+w\mathbf{c}$ with the integer coefficients $[uvw]$ constituting the direction symbol.  From a mathematical point of view we can regard each lattice node to be an infinitesimal point so we can represent it by a Dirac delta function; the complete 3D lattice, $\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})$, is then the collection of nodes represented by the following sum of delta functions:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{u,v,w=-\infty}^{+\infty} \delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{t}_{uvw})\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:directlattice}
\end{equation}
To generate a crystal structure we must copy the contents of the unit cell at each lattice node. If we represent the charge density in a single cell by $\rho_{\text{cell}}(\mathbf{r})$, then we can employ the convolution operation to copy the cell to each lattice point:
\begin{equation}
	\rho(\mathbf{r})=\rho_{\text{cell}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{u,v,w=-\infty}^{+\infty} \rho_{\text{cell}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{t}_{uvw})\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:latticewithcell}
\end{equation}
The charge density of the infinite crystal has the same translational periodicity as the underlying lattice, $\mathcal{T}$, so that it also has the same symmetry in the form of the space group. 

The first question we need to answer is the following: what is the Fourier transform of the direct lattice?  To answer this question we need to first know what the Fourier transform of the Dirac delta function looks like.  One can show that the following Fourier transform pair holds:
\begin{equation}
	\delta(\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{F}[1]=\iiint\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\quad\text{ and }\quad \delta(\mathbf{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[1]=\iiint\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:deltaFT}
\end{equation}
The integrals are taken over all of the respective spaces. If we consider the first relation, then for constant $\mathbf{k}$ the vector $\mathbf{r}$ will take on all possible values, and the resulting trigonometric functions will average to zero for each $\mathbf{r}$; the only exception is $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}$ for which the exponential is equal to unity, which integrates to an infinite value in that single point, i.e., we have a delta function $\delta(\mathbf{k})$.  

Next we can consider the Fourier transform of the lattice:
\[
	\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})]=\sum_{u,v,w=-\infty}^{+\infty} \mathcal{F}[\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{t}_{uvw})]=\sum_{u,v,w=-\infty}^{+\infty} \iiint \delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{t}_{uvw})\, \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\ .
\]
When the delta function is inside an integral, it has the property that it selects the rest of the integrand at the position of the delta peak; as a result we find:
\[
	\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})]=\sum_{u,v,w=-\infty}^{+\infty} \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{uvw}}\ .
\]	
Once again, the trigonometric functions will cancel each other out except when the exponential is equal to $1$, which occurs whenever $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{uvw}$ is an integer; at those locations the infinite sum diverges.  We know from crystallography that $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{uvw}$ is an integer only when $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$, i.e. we have a delta function at each reciprocal lattice point.  Therefore we have:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})]=\sum_{hkl=-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{g}_{hkl})=\mathcal{T}^{\ast}(\mathbf{q})\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:recipdelta}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}^{\ast}$ is the reciprocal lattice.  We have thus shown that the Fourier transform of the direct lattice is the reciprocal lattice.  

Since real samples always have a finite shape, we need to truncate the direct lattice from equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:directlattice}), so that there are no lattice nodes outside of the sample. We can achieve this by means of the \indexit{shape function}, $D(\mathbf{r})$, which is equal to $1$ inside the sample and $0$ outside.  The finite lattice is then represented by the product:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{T}_{\text{finite}}(\mathbf{r})=D(\mathbf{r})\,\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:finitelattice}
\end{equation}
We use the \indexit{Multiplication Theorem}, equation~(\ref{FOURIE:eq:FTmulttheorem}) to obtain:
\[
	\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}_{\text{finite}}(\mathbf{r})]=\mathcal{F}[D(\mathbf{r})\,\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})]=\mathcal{F}[D(\mathbf{r})]\otimes\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{r})]\ .
\]
Introducing the \indexit{shape amplitude}, $D(\mathbf{q})$, as the Fourier transform of the shape function, we obtain the final result:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}_{\text{finite}}(\mathbf{r})]=D(\mathbf{q})\otimes\mathcal{T}^{\ast}(\mathbf{q})\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:finiteFT}
\end{equation}
In words, the Fourier transform of a finite crystal lattice is obtained by copying the shape amplitude at each reciprocal lattice point; these points are hence no longer infinitesimal points but, instead, acquire a shape that is the Fourier transform of the sample shape.
