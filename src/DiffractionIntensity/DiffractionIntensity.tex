\section{Introduction}\label{\chabbr:sec:introduction}

In this chapter, we will describe the geometry of the diffraction process in a way that includes the most commonly used types of incident beams: x-rays, electrons, and neutrons. The primary goal of a diffraction experiment is to determine how atoms or molecules are arranged in 3D.  When we think about a crystal structure, then the primary unknowns are: the lattice parameters $\{a,b,c,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}$, the symmetry (point group and space group), the atom positions in terms of fractional coordinates in the unit cell, and, in some cases, the orientation of the lattice with respect to an external reference frame.  Diffraction techniques allow us to determine all of these quantities.  

In chapter~\ref{SCATTR:ch:WaveScattering}, we introduced several important concepts that we will make extensive use of in the present chapter: 
\begin{itemize}
\item wavelength, $\lambda$, and wave vector, $\mathbf{k}$;
\item atomic scattering factors, $f^X(s)$; electron scattering factors $f^e(s)$; neutron scattering lengths $b$;
\item the probability of scattering from the incident beam direction $\mathbf{k}_0$ to a direction $\mathbf{k}$ at an angle $2\theta$ is of the form:
\[
	P_{\mathbf{k}_0\rightarrow\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = f(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\Delta\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
\]
where $f(s)$ is any one of the scattering factors listed above and $\Delta\mathbf{l}=\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_0$.  The complex exponential accounts for the atom position $\mathbf{r}$ with respect to the origin and is known as a \indexit{phase factor}.
\end{itemize}
The \indexit{Debye Scattering Equation} provides a way to compute the scattered intensity for an arbitrary arrangement of atoms.
