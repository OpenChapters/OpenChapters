\section{Introductory Remarks}\label{\chabbr:sec:intro}

\begin{floatingfigure}[r]{3.2in}
\centering
{\includegraphics[width=3.0in]{tetrahedron.pdf}}
{\caption{\label{\chabbr:sec:tetrahedron}\small Graphical representation of the materials paradigm as a tetrahedron.}}
\end{floatingfigure}
\noindent It is not uncommon for the material scientist to describe their discipline in terms of a \indexit{tetrahedron}.  Each corner of the materials tetrahedron (Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:sec:tetrahedron}) represents one of the four cornerstones of the field: \textit{Microstructure, Properties, Processing}, and \textit{Performance}. Many materials scientists/engineers believe that optimization of material properties and performance through judicious control of processing parameters to obtain the desired microstructure is the central paradigm of the discipline.  Blacksmiths represent the original (empirical) practitioners of this approach; they vary the microstructure of steels by varying their heat treatment in order to affect their mechanical properties.  In the present day, we control the doping of semiconductors to make quantum dots, resulting in highly efficient light emitting diodes; this is phase separation\index{phase separation} to make a two-phase structure in which the size of the approximately spherical particles of one phase is small enough that quantum effects become important.

On the one hand, we have material properties, such as: strength, toughness, formability, conductivity, corrosion resistance, piezoelectricity, magnetic permeability, $\ldots$ On the other hand we have microstructural features, such as: grain size, grain shape, phase structure, composite structure, chemical composition (alloying), crystal structure, defect structure (e.g., porosity), and many others. It is one of the primary goals of the field of materials science and engineering to explicitly describe the links between properties and microstructure and to exploit them to optimize the performance of materials in real-life applications.\\  

\subsection{Microstructure\label{\chabbr:ssec:microstructures}}
\indexit{Microstructure} refers to the internal structure of a material, at various length scales.  Biology was revolutionized when \indexit{Leeuwenhoek} and others started to use optical microscopes to look at the internal structure of plants.  They were able to relate many characteristics of plants to their cell structure, for example.  Similarly, in what is now the materials field, \indexit{Sorby} was one of the first to make cross-sections of materials such as iron and examine them in the microscope, so that he could relate properties to structure.  Microstructure originally meant the structure inside a material that could be observed with the aid of an optical microscope; in the metric system, with its prefixes for units, the micrometer ($1$ $\mu$m $= 10^{-6}$ m) happens to correspond roughly to the wavelength of light.  Light obviously is used to form images in a light/optical microscope.  Thus, microstructure has come to be accepted to cover those elements of structure with a length scale on the order of $1$ $\mu$m.  Given microstructure at the $\mu$m scale, naturally some refer to \textit{nanostructure}\index{nanostructure} at the nm ($=10^{-9}$ m) scale.  Many important material properties are determined at the atomistic length scale. The continuum scale means, in the context of engineering, length scales large enough that materials can be treated as having homogeneous properties.

Most observable elements of microstructure are discontinuities, or \indexit{defects}, in the material.  Some of the most important defects include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \indexit{grain boundaries}: discontinuities in the crystal lattice and correspond to abrupt changes in the orientation of the crystal lattice;
	\item \indexit{phase boundaries}:  discontinuities in composition and, commonly, in crystal structure;
	\item \indexit{dislocations}:  local discontinuities in the lattice translational periodicity; they require observations in the nanoscale regime but their combined effect becomes noticeable at the microstructure level;
	\item \indexit{point defects}: missing atoms (vacancies)\index{vacancy} or extra (interstitial)\index{interstitial} atoms; they require specialized equipment to detect their presence but their effect can also be felt at the microstructure level.
\end{itemize}

Direct observation of microstructures requires us to make \textit{images}. In order of increasing effort, the standard methods are (1) \indexit{optical microscopy} (OM), (2) \indexit{scanning electron microscopy} (SEM),  (3) \indexit{scanning probe microscopy} (SPM), and (4) \indexit{transmission electron microscopy} (TEM).  Microscopies that rely on topographic contrast require specimen preparation in order to reveal the microstructure.  Metallography/ceramography\index{metallography}\index{ceramography} is the art of specimen preparation for microscopy.  The aim in specimen preparation is always to maximize contrast for the microstructural elements of interest while minimizing image artifacts.\footnote{Reminder: not all imaging methods require topographic relief.  Channeling contrast in the SEM uses variations in crystallographic orientation to affect image brightness giving a gray-scale image of grain structure, for example.}

It is essential to quantify microstructure in order to be able to predict properties quantitatively; what needs to be quantified depends on the property, i.e., what question is asked of the material. Examples of quantitative microstructural parameters are: grain size, void fraction, second phase particle size, $\ldots$.  Before discussing specific techniques, we must point out that the discipline of \indexit{stereology} is essential.  Most real materials are three dimensional, whereas most characterization methods provide information from either a planar section or from a surface. Even TEM requires thin foils (about $0.1$ $\mu$m thick or less). Therefore, some more sophisticated analysis is required to extract the true 3D quantities of interest. Modern materials characterization techniques include approaches to reconstruct the full 3D microstructure of a material, and this can be done at various length scales:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Atomic scale}:  the  3-D  atom probe\index{3D atom probe} \cite{seidman2000a,miller2004a,hono2005a} can generate maps of the locations of large numbers ($10^6$--$10^8$) of individual atoms in a relatively short time. The technique relies on field evaporation of individual atoms from a sharp tip placed in a strong electric field, and subsequent position and mass sensitive detection of these atoms.  This technique is limited to volumes of a few hundred cubic nanometers, and is very demanding in terms of sample preparation, but results in the ultimate resolution, since each atom is identified with respect to both chemistry and position.

\item \textit{``several cubic microns''}:  Focused ion beam (FIB)\index{focused ion beam}\index{FIB} serial sectioning techniques \cite{dunn1999a,uchic2006a} are used to analyze the 3-D  structure of polycrystalline samples with potentially complex phase mixtures.  When combined with \indexit{electron backscatter diffraction} and \indexit{energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy}, orientational and chemical information can be acquired at the same time.  This technique is typically limited to fractions of a cubic millimeter.  More recently, \indexit{femto-second laser ablation} \cite{Echlin2015} has been used to remove larger volumes of material at a much faster rate.

\item \textit{``several cubic millimeters''}: Automated serial sectioning methods based on milling \cite{alkemper2001a} or standard metallography \cite{spowart2003a} can generate  3-D  data from macroscopic samples. Combining these approaches  with \indexit{Laue diffraction} and \indexit{x-ray fluorescence} allows for the near-simultaneous acquisition of chemical and orientational information.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Properties}
\label{\chabbr:ssec:properties}

A material's \indexit{properties} are those measurable characteristics that determine how the material responds to a variety of external stimuli; we will formalize this statement in section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:matprop}. Broad property categories and specific examples in each category include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \indexit{mechanical properties}: strength, hardness, ductility, toughness, fatigue resistance;
\item \indexit{thermal properties}: conductivity, heat capacity, thermal expansion;
\item \indexit{electrical and magnetic properties}: conductivity, resistivity, ferromagnetism, superconductivity, band gap;
\item \indexit{optical properties}: transparency, reflectivity, photoluminescence;
\item \indexit{chemical properties}: corrosion resistance, oxidation behavior, reactivity.
\end{itemize}

We distinguish between properties that are \indexit{intrinsic} and \indexit{extrinsic}. Intrinsic properties are generally determined by the crystal structure (i.e., the 3D arrangement and types of atoms) and in many cases they can be computed/predicted directly from the atomic arrangements using physics-based arguments, in particular quantum-mechanical models.  The electronic \indexit{band gap} of a material is  a typical example of an intrinsic property; it is fundamental to the particular crystal structure and represents the minimum energy needed to promote an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, thereby determining whether the material is an insulator, a conductor, or a semiconductor.  \textit{Ductility}\index{ductility} is an example of an extrinsic property since it depends in a very sensitive way on microstructural parameters, such as the grain size, or the rate at which the material is deformed (i.e., the \indexit{strain rate}).  In general, such extrinsic properties are much more difficult to predict since they depend on many factors that themselves may be difficult to quantify, and improving one extrinsic property, say strength, may have a negative effect on another property, in this case ductility.  The often complex and counter-acting dependencies between microstructure and (extrinsic) properties make it necessary to systematically approach material properties by starting from the single crystal state, and then gradually incorporating the actual microstructural aspects by means of appropriate averaging procedures, which represents a very active area of current research.

\subsection{Processing}
\label{\chabbr:ssec:processing}

Processing describes not only how we make a material (e.g., by solidification from a melt pool) but also how we subsequently modify the material's microstructure, for instance via thermal annealing or plastic deformation, both belonging to the category of \indexit{thermomechanical processing} techniques.    Each material class (metals, ceramics, polymers, $\ldots$) comes with its own set of specialized processing techniques, but the common goal of all material processing techniques is to achieve a microstructure that will satisfy all performance criteria or metrics.   Every processing step, whether heating, cooling, deforming, or applying some other form of energy, affects the large scale arrangement of atoms and the evolution of microstructure. In metals, solidification from the melt establishes the initial grain structure; plastic deformation by rolling or forging introduces dislocations; subsequent heat treatments can reorganize those dislocations and thereby affect the mechanical performance of the material.

Processing is not limited to traditional metallurgy. In ceramics, careful control of sintering temperature and atmosphere determines porosity and grain growth. In polymers, extrusion and drawing can align chains and improve strength. In electronic materials, epitaxial growth techniques deposit atomically precise thin films. More recently, additive manufacturing methods, such as laser powder bed fusion, are offering unprecedented control by  printing parts layer by layer. A key teaching point is that processing is a lever: by changing it, we can intentionally engineer microstructures, and therefore tune material properties.  One of the central questions that occupies the modern materials scientist or engineer is  the following: is it possible to predict the processing steps that will lead to a desired performance?


\subsection{Performance}
\label{\chabbr:ssec:performance}

Performance takes the discussion out of the lab and into the real world. It asks the question: How does the material actually behave in service, under realistic conditions and over long time scales? A material may exhibit excellent tensile strength in a simple test, but if it corrodes rapidly in a marine environment, its performance as a structural material for ships will be poor. Similarly, a ceramic may have outstanding hardness, but if it is too brittle, it cannot perform well in an application involving repeated impact.

Performance thus integrates not just the intrinsic properties of the material but also the environment, loading conditions, time, and cost. Engineers must consider fatigue (failure after many cycles of stress), creep (slow deformation at high temperature), wear, corrosion, and radiation damage. Performance also includes economic and societal factors, whether the material is sustainable, affordable, and available at scale.

In teaching, performance is often presented as the close-the-loop element of the MSE framework. Microstructure is tailored by processing, which defines properties, which then dictate performance. This cycle emphasizes that materials are not just studied for their own sake but for their essential role in enabling advanced technologies such as jet engines, medical implants, solar cells, cell phones, bridges, and countless others.


\section{Definition of a Material Property}\label{\chabbr:sec:matprop}

%\begin{messagebox}{{\sffamily\bfseries Important Section}}{ocre}{\icattention}{OCwhite}
%In this section you will learn a formal definition of what a material property is.  Later on in this chapter we will introduce many examples.
%\end{messagebox}

Although it is difficult to provide a general abstract description of a material property,\footnote{The content of this chapter is based on a number of books dedicated to the tensor properties of solids; the most important references used are \cite{kiral1990a,nowick1995a,white1999a,newnham2004a,tinder2007a}.} valid for all possible properties, it is instructive to think of a material property as \textit{the link between an external influence and the material's response to that influence}.  If we denote the external influence by $\mathcal{F}$ ($\mathcal{F}$ stands for \underline{F}ield or \underline{F}orce) and the material \underline{R}esponse or \underline{R}eaction by $\mathcal{R}$, then in the most general sense the relation between the two is given by
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}),\label{\chabbr:eq:rf}
\end{equation}
or, put in words, the material response is a function of the externally applied field.  It is one of the tasks of a materials scientist to figure out and understand what that response function looks like.

We know from calculus that, for ``well-behaved'' functions, we can always expand the function into powers of its argument. For equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:rf}) above, the \indexit{Taylor expansion} around $\mathcal{F}=0$, i.e., the \indexit{McLaurin series}, is given by:
\begin{align}
	\mathcal{R}&=\mathcal{R}_0 + 
	\frac{1}{1!}\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}}\right 
	\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}\!\!\!\!\mathcal{F} + 
	\frac{1}{2!}\left.\frac{\partial^2\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}^2}\right 
	\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}\!\!\!\!\mathcal{F}^2 + 
	\frac{1}{3!}\left.\frac{\partial^3\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}^3}\right 
	\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}\!\!\!\!\mathcal{F}^3 + \ldots \nonumber\\
	&= \mathcal{R}_0 +
	\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left.\frac{\partial^n\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}^n}\right 
	\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}\!\!\!\!\mathcal{F}^n,
	\label{\chabbr:eq:expansion}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{R}_0 = \mathcal{R}(0)=\mathcal{R}\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}$ describes the ``state'' of the material at zero field.  There are two possibilities for $\mathcal{R}_0$:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item $\mathcal{R}_0=0$: in the absence of an external field ($\mathcal{F}=0$), there is no permanent (or \indexit{remanent}) material response.  For example, if the external field is an applied stress, and the material response is a strain, then at zero stress there is no strain (assuming linear elasticity).  Or, if the applied field is an electric field, and the response is an electrical current, then at zero field, no current flows (unless the material is in a superconducting state).
	
	\item $\mathcal{R}_0\neq 0$: in the absence of an external field ($\mathcal{F}=0$), there is a permanent material response.  For example, in a ferromagnetic material the magnetization is in general different from zero, even at zero applied field (i.e., a permanent magnet).  
\end{enumerate}

If we truncate the series after the second term (i.e., we ignore all derivatives of $\mathcal{R}$ except for the first one), then the expression for $\mathcal{R}$ becomes rather simple:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_0 + 
	\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}}\right 
	\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}\!\!\!\!\mathcal{F} =\mathcal{R}_0 + 
	\mathbf{P}\mathcal{F}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad \mathbf{P}\equiv\left.  
	\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}}\right\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0.
	}
\end{equation}
This is a \textit{linear} relation between the applied field and the response, and this relation becomes a proportionality between applied field and response when $\mathcal{R}_0=0$.  The quantity $\mathbf{P}$ is a \textit{material property}.\index{material property}  Ignoring the higher order derivatives of $\mathcal{R}$ is generally known as the \textit{linear approximation}.\index{linear approximation}  This approximation considerably simplifies the math and for many purposes it is a useful and accurate approximation.

Since the external field $\mathcal{F}$ is often a vector quantity (e.g., the electric field $\mathcal{F}=\mathbf{E}$) and the response can also be a vector (e.g., the current density $\mathcal{R}=\mathbf{j}$) it is clear that the material property $\mathbf{P}$ is not always going to be describable by a single number.  Instead, the material property can become a vector itself, or even a higher order mathematical quantity known as a \indexit{tensor}; nearly all of the interesting and useful material properties are represented by tensors, in particular for single crystals but also in polycrystalline materials.  For now, think of a tensor as an array of numbers with a set of special properties; in the Foundational Chapter~\ref{TENSOR:ch:Tensors} the concept of a tensor is described in substantial detail.  Without going into the details at this time, we can state that, in general, a material property is represented by a \textit{matrix} of numbers; this can be a column vector, a $3\times 3$ matrix or even a higher dimensional array.  A combination of thermodynamic (or intrinsic) symmetry and the crystallographic symmetry of the material determines which of those numbers can be different from zero, and which \textit{must} be zero.  

If the series expansion in equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:expansion}) is truncated after the third term, then it is said that the material behaves in a non-linear way.  The relation can then be written as:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_0 + \mathbf{P}\mathcal{F} + 
	\mathbf{P}_2\mathcal{F}^2\quad\mbox{ with }\quad \mathbf{P}_2\equiv\frac{1}{2!}\left.\frac{\partial^2\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathcal{F}^2}\right 
	\vert_{\mathcal{F}=0}
\end{equation}
$\mathbf{P}_2$ is a \textit{non-linear} material property.  Crystallography and symmetry theory can again be used to examine the relations between the components of $\mathbf{P}_2$.  
	
There are several material properties for which it is impossible to truncate the Taylor expansion of equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:expansion}) after just a few terms, and many or all terms must be used. For those properties, it is customary to use equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:rf}), with some appropriate mathematical function $\mathcal{R}$. In that case, equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:rf}) represents a so-called \indexit{constitutive law} or \indexit{constitutive relation}.  Sometimes, physical arguments can be used to find the precise form of the function, in other situations empirical or phenomenological relations may be needed.

One can also express the \textit{energy content} of a material in terms of the externally applied field(s); \textit{thermodynamics}\index{thermodynamics} then provides the means and rules to determine how the energy of a solid/liquid/gas changes when the external conditions change.  Thermodynamics often assumes that a material system is \textit{homogeneous} and \textit{isotropic} (see below) and then makes quite general statements about its behavior in various external fields.  Symmetry becomes important whenever a thermodynamic function depends on the gradient of a property; in such cases, symmetry theory can determine the possible mathematical expressions that will correctly describe this property.  There is thus an intimate relation between the symmetry of a material and its thermodynamic properties.  Whereas symmetry theory determines which elements of a property matrix must be zero, thermodynamics can be used to derive additional restrictions on the non-zero elements (e.g., some elements can only be positive, or certain elements must always be smaller than others, etc.)  

Every material property has a number of characteristics\footnote{We could call them \textit{properties} but it is a bit confusing to talk about the properties of a property.} which are of fundamental importance: \textit{homogeneity, anisotropy} and \textit{symmetry}; let us take a brief closer look at each of these concepts.

\subsection{Homogeneity}
\label{\chabbr:ssec:homogeneity}

There are several length scales at which we can make observations on a material.  Macroscopic measurements are considered to be those measurements that are performed over distances many times larger than the interatomic distance, e.g.\
\begin{eqnarray}
	\mbox{length : }&&L \gg a; \nonumber\\
	\mbox{surface : }&&S \gg a^{2};\\
	\mbox{volume : }&&V \gg a^{3},\nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
where $a$ is of the order of the interatomic distance.  The majority of objects with which we deal in everyday life are composed of several different \textit{materials}: metals, plastics, wood, brick, and many others.  At the macroscopic level, most of those materials may appear to consist of only one piece.  However, when we use a microscope to zoom in on the details, we can often observe that the material is made up of small \textit{grains}.  Each of those grains is an individual crystal, usually with a rather irregular shape.  When we zoom in even further, we can observe the \textit{crystal lattice}, which shows a regular arrangement of atoms.  If a material consists of many grains in more or less random orientations, then we refer to that material as \textit{poly-crystalline}.  If the complete object is formed by only one grain, then that object is a \textit{single crystal}; many gems are single crystals.  It is customary to concentrate first on the properties of single crystals, and subsequently determine how a deviation from the single crystal state affects those properties.  To obtain a deeper understanding about the way materials work it is necessary to start at the microscopic or \textit{crystallographic} level and work our way up to real materials.

A crystalline substance is \textit{homogeneous}\index{homogeneous} when all physical (e.g.\ optical, mechanical, etc.)  and physicochemical (e.g.\ solubility of surface, adsorption, etc.)  properties are the same for volume (or surface) elements at different locations in the substance.  If a material point is denoted by the coordinate vector $\mathbf{r}$, then the mathematical statement of homogeneity of a property $\mathbf{P}$ becomes :
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}\,)=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\,\prime})
\end{equation}
where the points $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{r}^{\,\prime}$ are separated by a (random) macroscopic distance.  The property $\mathbf{P}$ can be a scalar (heat capacity, density, etc.), a vector (polarization, magnetization, etc.)  or a higher order quantity known as a tensor (elastic moduli, magnetic susceptibility, etc.). \textit{Homogeneity} thus requires that a material property be independent of the location in the material where it is measured.  If a property \textit{does} depend upon location, then it is said that the property is \indexit{heterogeneous}.  For instance, a natural sapphire (Al$_2$O$_3$) crystal may contain Cr atoms; if the distribution of Cr atoms is homogeneous throughout the sample, then the color of the crystal will be independent of location.  However, if one side of the crystal contains more Cr than the other side, then there is a \textit{compositional gradient} across the sample, and the color may vary with location.  For many materials applications homogeneity is a strict requirement; for instance, for the cables used in suspension bridges, we want the mechanical properties to be the same along the length of the cables. For many other applications gradients or fluctuations are required;  for instance, if a semiconductor device were chemically homogeneous, it would completely fail to function.

\subsection{Anisotropy}
\label{\chabbr:ssec:anisotropy}

A homogeneous property can be either isotropic or anisotropic in a single crystal.  If a property is the same in every direction, then that property is said to be \textit{isotropic},\index{isotropic} otherwise it is \textit{anisotropic}.\index{anisotropic}  Every isotropic homogeneous quantity must necessarily be described by a \textit{scalar}.  The most interesting properties are often those which are direction dependent.  In Foundational Chapter~\ref{TENSOR:ch:Tensors} we describe in detail how the mathematical quantity known as a tensor is perfectly suited for the description of anisotropic behavior.  Examples of properties which may depend upon direction include thermal conductivity, dielectric and magnetic susceptibilities, elastic moduli, and so on.  It is important to note that an anisotropic property in a single crystal can become isotropic in a poly-crystalline material, since all crystal orientations may be simultaneously present in the poly-crystal.

\subsection{Symmetry}\label{\chabbr:ssec:symmetry}

Symmetry is one of the more fundamental properties of crystalline matter.  Apart from color, it is also in many cases the most ``eye-catching'' aspect of a natural crystal.  One can introduce the concept as follows: take an arbitrary object and determine if there is a way to ``move'' the object into another orientation in such a way that the initial and final configurations are indistinguishable.  The specific ways of ``moving'' an object into \textit{self-coincidence} represent the symmetries of that object.  Enumeration of the different symmetry properties of a crystal forms the subject of the ``group theory'' of crystallography.  All possible crystallographic symmetry operations and their combinations into point groups and space groups are described in detail in Chapter~\ref{CRYSYM:ch:Symmetry}.


\section{Representation of a Material Property by a Tensor}\label{\chabbr:sec:proptensor}

\subsection{Why do we use tensors?}\label{\chabbr:ssec:whytensors}
In the Foundational Chapter~\ref{TENSOR:ch:Tensors} on tensors, the general notion of a tensor is described in a somewhat formal way.  A tensor is \textit{a mathematical object that transforms in a precisely defined way when going from one reference frame to another}. In particular, a tensor transforms as the \indexit{direct product} (or \indexit{tensor product}) between two or more vectors.  Just as a vector has a length and a direction, both independent of any reference frame, a tensor also \textit{exists} as an object, independent of any reference frame.  As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:frames}(a), when an actual reference frame is selected (we will call this the ``old'' reference frame $\{\mathbf{e}_i\}$), then the components of the vector $\mathbf{p}$ can be determined from orthogonal projections onto the basis vectors of that reference frame (represented by the small blue circles).  When a different reference frame is selected (Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:frames}(b) and (c)), the ``new'' reference frames $\{\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_i\}$ and $\{\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}_i\}$, then the vector components will transform in such a way that the vector itself does not change; we say that the vector is \indexit{frame invariant}.  The same is true for the 3D case, but there are many more possible orientations for the reference frame since there are three degrees of freedom for the rotations instead of just one for the 2D case.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering\leavevmode
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\graphicspath frames.pdf}
\caption{\label{\chabbr:fig:frames}Three different reference frames to describe the same 2D vector. The vector components are different in each frame, but they all describe the same vector.}
\end{figure}

More formally, if the matrix $\alpha_{ij}$ represents the rotation that takes the old basis vectors $\{\mathbf{e}_j\}$ (Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:frames}(a)) into the new vectors $\{\mathbf{e}'_i\}$ (Fig.~\ref{\chabbr:fig:frames}(b)), then we can write, using the summation convention:
\[
	\mathbf{e}'_i = \alpha_{ij}\mathbf{e}_j\ .
\]
As explained in detail in chapter~\ref{TENSOR:ch:Tensors}, this relation leads to the following transformation rules for vector and tensor components (tensor ranks $1$, $2$, and $3$ are shown here as illustrations):
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
	v'_i &= \alpha_{ij}v_j\ ;\\
	\sigma'_{ij} &= \alpha_{ik}\alpha_{jl}\sigma_{kl}\ ;\\
	\tau'_{ijk} &= \alpha_{il}\alpha_{jm}\alpha_{kn}\tau_{lmn}\ .
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
It is a general rule in physics that all equations must be frame-invariant, i.e., they must  have the same form for all observers. In other words, Newton's first law, $\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}$, has the same form for all observers, but the components of the vectors will depend on the particular reference frame selected to describe the problem.  The same is true for the materials field; when we describe a particular property tensor, for instance the \indexit{photoelasticity} tensor $\gamma_{ijkl}$, a tensor of rank $4$, then we must also explicitly specify which reference frame was used to express the tensor coefficients. Since, in many cases, properties are described for crystalline solids, it stands to reason that the reference frame selected to describe the components of $\gamma_{ijkl}$ will be related to the crystal structure itself.  In section~\ref{BASCRY:sec:Cartesian}, we defined a standard Cartesian reference frame that is valid for all crystal systems; this reference frame is what most researchers will use when expressing the components of material property tensors.

\subsection{Example Tensor Property: the Electrical Conductivity Tensor}\label{\chabbr:ssec:sigma}

The properties of a material must be independent of the reference frame we select to describe them.  Frame-independence allows us to write general expressions, such as the relation between an applied electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and the resulting electrical current density $\mathbf{j}$~:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{j}=\bm{\sigma}\mathbf{E}\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:conductivity}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\sigma}$ is the electrical conductivity tensor.  
The form of this equation does not depend on a specific reference frame; it is the same in all reference  frames. The equation describes a relation between two vectors and those vectors exist, regardless of the presence or absence of a reference frame.  \

If a single crystal is isotropic, then the electrical conductivity $\sigma$ is the same in all directions and we have:
\[
	\mathbf{j}=\sigma\mathbf{E}\qquad\mbox{isotropic material}\ .
\]
Therefore, the two vectors $\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{E}$ are parallel.  For some crystals, however, the current density does depend upon the direction of the electric field vector and the two vectors \textit{need not be parallel.} This means that the electrical conductivity can no longer be represented by a simple number $\sigma$.  The most general linear relation between the two vectors can be  written as~:
\begin{eqnarray}
j_x &=& \sigma_{xx} E_x + \sigma_{xy} E_y + \sigma_{xz} E_z\nonumber\\
j_y &=& \sigma_{yx} E_x + \sigma_{yy} E_y + \sigma_{yz} E_z\\
j_z &=& \sigma_{zx} E_x + \sigma_{zy} E_y + \sigma_{zz} E_z\nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
For the anisotropic material we thus need 9 numbers to describe the electrical conductivity instead of a single number for the isotropic material (actually, we will see later that only six of these components are independent of each other).  We can introduce a $3\times 3$ matrix with elements $\sigma_{ij}$ to describe these nine numbers.  If we denote that matrix by $\bm{\sigma}$, then the frame-invariance requires $\bm{\sigma}$ to be a tensor, in this case a second rank tensor, and the relations above can be written in index notation as:
\[
	j_i = \sigma_{ij} E_j\ ,
\]
where the vector and tensor components refer to the (old) reference frame $\mathbf{e}_j$.  In a new reference frame, the relation must read:
\[
	j'_i = \sigma'_{ij} E'_j\ ,
\]
and we have the transformation relations $j'_i=\alpha_{ik}j_k$, $E'_j=\alpha_{jk}E_k$, for the vector quantities and $\sigma'_{ij}=\alpha_{ik}\alpha_{jl}\sigma_{kl}$ for the second-rank tensor because of the requirement for frame-invariance.

\subsection{Example Tensor Transformation for the Electrical Conductivity Tensor}
In this section we provide an example of a tensor transformation using the electrical conductivity tensor $\bm{\sigma}$. Let us assume that, in the old reference frame $\{\mathbf{e}_i\}$, the components of the electrical conductivity tensor for a hypothetical material are given by~:
\[
\sigma_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & 0 & \sigma_3\\
0 & \sigma_1 & 0\\
\sigma_3 & 0 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix}\ .
\]
If we also select a particular electric field vector $\mathbf{E}$, say~:
\[
E_i=\begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}\ ,
\]
then we can compute the resulting electrical current density $\mathbf{j}$ as~:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & 0 & \sigma_3\\
0 & \sigma_1 & 0\\
\sigma_3 & 0 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}  = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sigma_1\\ \sigma_1\\ 2\sigma_3\end{bmatrix} =\mathbf{j}\ .
\]
Note that this vector is clearly not parallel to $\mathbf{E}$. If the new reference frame, $\{\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_j\}$, is related to the old one by a 45$^{\circ}$ clockwise rotation around the common $\mathbf{e}_z$ axis, then the transformation matrix $\alpha$ will be given by~:
\[
	\alpha_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}c & s & 0\\ -s & c & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\text{ with } c=s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.
\] 
The transformed vector components are then readily shown to be:
\[
	j'_i = \begin{pmatrix}c & s & 0\\ -s & c & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2\sigma_1\\ \sigma_1\\ 2\sigma_3\end{bmatrix} = 
	\begin{bmatrix} (2c+s)\sigma_1\\ (c-2s)\sigma_1\\ 2\sigma_3\end{bmatrix}\ ,
\]
and 
\[
	E'_j = \begin{pmatrix}c & s & 0\\ -s & c & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1\\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2c+s\\ c-2s\\ 0\end{bmatrix}\ .
\]
For the conductivity tensor, the transformation rule is given by the double matrix product:
\[
	\sigma^{\prime}_{pk}= \alpha_{pi}\sigma_{ij} \widetilde{\alpha}_{jk}\ ,
\]
which leads to 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
	\sigma^{\prime}_{pk}&= \begin{pmatrix}c & s & 0\\ -s & c & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & 0 & \sigma_3\\
0 & \sigma_1 & 0\\
\sigma_3 & 0 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}c & -s & 0\\ s & c & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\ ;\\
&= \begin{pmatrix}c & s & 0\\ -s & c & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}  \begin{bmatrix}c\sigma_1 & -s\sigma_1 & \sigma_3\\
s\sigma_1 & c\sigma_1 & 0\\
c\sigma_3 & -s\sigma_3 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix}\ ;\\
&= \begin{bmatrix}(c^2+s^2)\sigma_1 & 0 & c\sigma_3\\
0 & (c^2+s^2)\sigma_1 & -s\sigma_3\\
c\sigma_3 & -s\sigma_3 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & 0 & c\sigma_3\\
0 & \sigma_1 & -s\sigma_3\\
c\sigma_3 & -s\sigma_3 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix}\ ;
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
in the last step we made use of the fact that $c^2+s^2=1$.  We can readily verify that:
\[
	j'_i = \sigma'_{ij}E'_j\rightarrow \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & 0 & c\sigma_3\\
0 & \sigma_1 & -s\sigma_3\\
c\sigma_3 & -s\sigma_3 & \sigma_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2c+s\\ c-2s\\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (2c+s)\sigma_1\\ (c-2s)\sigma_1\\ 2\sigma_3\end{bmatrix}\ ,
\]
as it should be.  By means of this example, we have shown that the tensor transformation relations applied to all factors of the equation $\mathbf{j}=\bm{\sigma}\mathbf{E}$  \textit{guarantee} that this equation remains valid in the new reference frame.  

As a more complex example, consider \indexit{Hooke's Law}, which states that stress is proportional to strain:
\[
	\sigma_{ij}=c_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl},
\]
where $\bm{\sigma}$ is the stress tensor, $\bm{\epsilon}$ the strain tensor, and $\mathbf{c}$ the elastic moduli tensor (a material property).  This law must be independent of the reference frame, so in the primed reference frame we must have:
\[
	\sigma'_{pq}=c'_{pqrs}\epsilon'_{rs}.
\]
For each individual quantity, we have to apply the standard tensor transformation relation to move between reference frames; if the transformation is defined by $\mathbf{e}'_i = \alpha_{ij}\mathbf{e}_j$, then we have for the second rank tensors:
\[
	\sigma'_{pq} = \alpha_{pi}\alpha_{qj}\sigma_{ij};
\] 
\[
	\epsilon'_{rs} = \alpha_{rk}\alpha_{sl}\epsilon_{kl};
\] 
and for the fourth rank elastic moduli tensor
\[
	c'_{pqrs} = \alpha_{pi}\alpha_{qj}\alpha_{rk}\alpha_{sl} c_{ijkl}
\]
Note that, in this last expression, a summation is implied over the indices $i$, $j$, $k$ and $l$, so that this 
expression is equivalent to
\[
	c'_{pqrs}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l=1}^{3}\alpha_{pi}\alpha_{qj}\alpha_{rk}\alpha_{sl} c_{ijkl}
\]
Although the indices $p$, $q$, $r$ and $s$ also appear twice, they do so on opposite sides of the equality sign, hence no summation is implied.  Note also that for tensors of rank higher than $2$, it is no longer possible to write the transformation in terms of matrix products; one must carry out the explicit summations for each tensor component.


\subsection{Crystallographic Symmetry and Material Property Tensors}\label{\chabbr:ssec:propsym}

In this section, we will analyze how crystallographic symmetry affects the properties of a material.  To make the treatment concrete, we will consider a simple second rank tensor, namely the \indexit{magnetic permeability tensor} $\bm{\mu}$, measured in units of Wb/(Am) or Henries per meter (H/m), which is the proportionality factor between an external applied magnetic field $\mathbf{H}$ and the material's resulting magnetic induction $\mathbf{B}$, i.e., $B_i=\mu_{ij}H_j$.  The procedure outlined below is valid for tensors of arbitrary rank, but becomes rather tedious for higher rank tensors. Group theory has very powerful theorems and techniques to make this task considerably easier, but the mathematics is beyond the level of the present chapter.

Let us assume that we have a tetragonal single crystal with a symmetry corresponding to point group \pg{9}, which has $4$ elements: $\textbf{4} = \{E,4,4^2,4^3\}$, i.e., it is a cyclic rotation group with the identity $E$, and powers of the fourfold rotation operator $4$. We know from Chapter~\ref{CRYSYM:ch:Symmetry} that each point symmetry operator can be represented by a $3\times 3$ matrix.  In this particular case, we have the following four matrices:
\begin{align}
E &= \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\ 0&1&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}; \qquad R_1 = 4 = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix};\nonumber\\
R_2 &= 4^2= \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\ 0&-1&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}; \qquad R_3 = 4^3 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\ -1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\ .
\end{align}
They can be derived quite easily by considering how each basis vector transforms under a rotation of $90^{\circ}$ around the $z$ axis.

If we apply the operator $4$ to the tetragonal crystal, then we basically carry out a rotation of $90^{\circ}$, and the resulting crystal is indistinguishable from the original after the rotation.  This must also be true for all the properties of this crystal.  There is a famous theorem, attributed to Neumann, that says the following:  \textit{The symmetry elements of any physical property of a crystal \textbf{must} include the symmetry elements of the point group of the crystal.}  This is known as \indexit{Neumann's principle}.  This principle states that every material property tensor must inherit the crystallographic symmetry of the underlying crystal lattice.  Note the use of the words \textit{must include}; this means that a material property may have a higher symmetry than the crystallographic symmetry, but never a lower symmetry.

We described in previous sections how the components of a tensor change under a coordinate transformation. Since a symmetry operation is a special case of a coordinate transformation, we can work out how each of the operators of the point group \pg{9} changes the components of the permeability tensor $\bm{\mu}$.  We take into account the fact that the permeability tensor itself is a symmetric tensor, i.e., $\mu_{ij}=\mu_{ji}$ (due to intrinsic thermodynamic symmetry, which we will introduce in section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:thermosym}), so that there are only $6$ independent elements to start with.
{\small\begin{align*}
	R_1\bm{\mu}\tilde{R}_1 &= \begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{xx}&\mu_{xy}&\mu_{xz}\\ \mu_{xy}&\mu_{yy}&\mu_{yz}\\ \mu_{xz}&\mu_{yz}&\mu_{zz}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\ -1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = 
	\left[\begin{array}{ccc} 
	 \mu_{yy} & -\mu_{xy} & -\mu_{yz} \\ 
	-\mu_{xy} &  \mu_{xx} & \mu_{xz} \\ 
	-\mu_{yz} &  \mu_{xz} & \mu_{zz}\end{array}\right]\ ;\\
	R_2\bm{\mu}\tilde{R}_2 &= \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\ 0&-1&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{xx}&\mu_{xy}&\mu_{xz}\\ \mu_{xy}&\mu_{yy}&\mu_{yz}\\ \mu_{xz}&\mu_{yz}&\mu_{zz}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\ 0&-1&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = 
	\left[\begin{array}{ccc} 
	 \mu_{xx} & \mu_{xy} & -\mu_{xz} \\ 
	\mu_{xy} &  \mu_{yy} & -\mu_{yz} \\ 
	-\mu_{xz} &  -\mu_{yz} & \mu_{zz}\end{array}\right]\ ;\\
	R_3\bm{\mu}\tilde{R}_3 &= \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\  -1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{xx}&\mu_{xy}&\mu_{xz}\\ \mu_{xy}&\mu_{yy}&\mu_{yz}\\ \mu_{xz}&\mu_{yz}&\mu_{zz}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = 
	\left[\begin{array}{ccc} 
	 \mu_{yy} & -\mu_{xy} & \mu_{yz} \\ 
	-\mu_{xy} &  \mu_{xx} & -\mu_{xz} \\ 
	\mu_{yz} &  -\mu_{xz} & \mu_{zz}\end{array}\right]\ .
\end{align*}}
Each of the tensors on the right-hand side of these equations must now be equal to the original tensor $\bm{\mu}$.  If we
compare components we find that $\mu_{xx}=\mu_{yy}$, and $\mu_{xy}=-\mu_{xy}$ which means that 
$\mu_{xy}=0$.  Repeating this analysis for all components, we find that the permeability tensor in a crystal with point
group $\textbf{4}$ \textit{must} have the following form:
\begin{equation}
	\mu_{ij} =  \left[\begin{array}{ccc} 
	\mu_{xx} & 0 & 0 \\ 
	0 &  \mu_{xx} & 0 \\ 
	0 & 0 & \mu_{zz}\end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc} 
	\mu_{1} & 0 & 0 \\ 
	0 &  \mu_{1} & 0 \\ 
	0 & 0 & \mu_{2}\end{array}\right]\ .
\end{equation}
In other words, there are only two independent elements in the permeability tensor.  The second notation is often used instead of the tensor subscript notation.

This simple derivation shows that crystallographic symmetry causes some tensor components to vanish; in a material with point group symmetry \pg{9}, the magnetic permeability coefficient $\mu_{xy}$ \textit{must} be zero, and $\mu_{xx}$ \textit{must} be equal to $\mu_{yy}$.  Note that it is possible for the material property to have a \textit{higher} symmetry than the crystal point group.  While the crystal point group \pg{9} does not contain the inversion operator, $C_i$, the permeability tensor is invariant under the inversion operation:
{\small\begin{equation}
C_i\bm{\mu}\tilde{C}_i = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\ 0&-1&0\\ 0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{xx}&\mu_{xy}&\mu_{xz}\\ \mu_{xy}&\mu_{yy}&\mu_{yz}\\ \mu_{xz}&\mu_{yz}&\mu_{zz}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\ 0&-1&0\\ 0&0&-1\end{pmatrix} = 
	\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{xx}&\mu_{xy}&\mu_{xz}\\ \mu_{xy}&\mu_{yy}&\mu_{yz}\\ \mu_{xz}&\mu_{yz}&\mu_{zz}\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}}
This is true for every second rank material tensor and for all point group symmetries.  This type of derivation can be repeated for all material property tensors of different ranks and all crystallographic point groups, and would be quite tedious if carried out by hand.  Fortunately, group theory has some advanced theorems that make this process substantially easier; the interested reader should consult dedicated group theory books, e.g., \cite{kiral90a,tinkham64a,mcweeny2002a,rose2003a}. 

The derivation above for point group \pg{9} reduced the number of independent components of the magnetic permeability tensor from $6$ to just $2$.  It is easy to show that for this symmetry, the permeability is isotropic in the plane normal to the $4$-fold rotation axis. If we take a rotation around the $z$ axis by an arbitrary clockwise angle $\alpha$, then the transformed tensor is given by:
\[
	\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha &\sin\alpha&0\\ -\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}	\mu_{1} & 0 & 0 \\ 
	0 &  \mu_{1} & 0 \\ 
	0 & 0 & \mu_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha &-\sin\alpha&0\\ \sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=
	\begin{bmatrix}	\mu_{1} & 0 & 0 \\ 
	0 &  \mu_{1} & 0 \\ 
	0 & 0 & \mu_{2}\end{bmatrix}\ ;
\]
in other words, the rotated tensor is identical to the original tensor, which means that the permeability is the same in any direction in the $(001)$ plane.  This is an example of a more general rule which states that, \textit{for a rotation axis of order $n$, any material tensor of order less than $n$ will be isotropic in the plane perpendicular to that axis}.  As a consequence of this rule, for cubic symmetry, with its multiple three-fold rotation axes, all second rank tensors must be fully isotropic, i.e., they can all be written as:
\[
	t_{ij} = t\delta_{ij}\ ,
\]
where the symbol $t_{ij}$ denotes an arbitrary rank-2 tensor.  In fact, for a material with point group symmetry \pg{32}, all property tensors of rank less than $4$ are reduced to scalars; tensors of rank $4$ and higher are the only tensors than can be anisotropic for this symmetry.


\subsection{Examples of Material Property Tensors}\label{\chabbr:sec:propertylist}
 
In this section, we will introduce a number of property tensors that are important for engineering materials applications.  We list the properties in order of increasing tensor rank starting with scalar properties.  For the applied fields we use the following notations [the SI units for each quantity are shown between square brackets]: 
 \begin{itemize}
 \item $\Delta T$ = temperature difference [K]; 
 \item $E_i$ = electric field components [V/m]; 
 \item $H_i$ = magnetic field components [A/m]; 
 \item $\sigma_{ij}$ = mechanical stress [Pa=N/m$^2$]; 
 \item $\nabla_j T$ = temperature gradient [K/m];
 \item $\nabla_j c$ = concentration gradient [kg/m$^4$]. 
 \end{itemize}
 
\noindent For the material responses we have: 
 \begin{itemize}
 \item $\Delta S$ = entropy change per unit mass [J/(K\,kg)]; 
 \item $D_i$ = electric displacement [C/m$^2$]; 
 \item $B_i$ = magnetic induction [T$=$Wb/m$^2$]; 
 \item $\epsilon_{ij}$ = mechanical strain [dimensionless]; 
 \item $j_i$ = electrical current density [A/m$^2$]; 
 \item $h_i$ = heat flux [W/m$^2$]; 
 \item $m_i$ = mass flux [kg/(m$^2$s)].  
 \end{itemize}
One should note that in some cases the roles of field and response can be exchanged. For instance, we have the relation $\mathbf{j}=\bm{\sigma}\mathbf{E}$ where $\bm{\sigma}$ is the electrical conductivity tensor, and its inverse form, $\mathbf{E}=\bm{\rho}\mathbf{j}$, where $\bm{\rho}$ is the \indexit{electrical resistivity} tensor. In principle, every property tensor has an inverse tensor which represents the proportionality factor when field and response are interchanged from the original definition.

Note also that three of the quantities defined above are gradient quantities: the electric field $\mathbf{E}=-\nabla\varphi$ is the gradient of the electrical potential, $\varphi(\mathbf{r})$, the temperature gradient  $\nabla_j T$ , and the thermal gradient $\nabla_j c$.  The responses to these gradients are flows: current flow $\mathbf{j}$, heat flow $\mathbf{h}$, and mass flow $\mathbf{m}$, respectively.  Collectively, we refer to the proportionality factors between these quantities as \indexit{transport properties}. The material properties that do not involve a flow or motion of particles are known as \indexit{equilibrium properties}.

The subsections below list the most important material property tensors in terms of the Field and Response that they connect. More details regarding the underlying physics as well as examples of engineering applications of these material properties can be found in other chapters.

\subsubsection{Property Tensors of Rank 0 (Scalars)}

Among the fields and responses listed above, only the temperature change, $\Delta T$, and the entropy change, $\Delta S$, are scalar quantities, so the proportionality factor is also a scalar quantity or a tensor of rank zero.  This is the \indexit{specific heat capacity} $C$ [J/(kg\,K)], which is defined as the amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of a unit mass $m$ of that material by exactly $1$ K; the resulting entropy change is then given by:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta S = C\,\frac{\Delta T}{T}\ .\label{\chabbr:eq:specificheat}
\end{equation}
Here and in the remainder of this chapter, $\Delta S$ refers to the entropy change per unit mass and has units [J/(K\,kg)].  Note that this relation is really only valid for small temperature changes; for large changes it must be replaced by an integration between the initial and final temperatures. The entropy change also depends on whether the measurement is made at constant pressure or in a constant volume closed system.  

As stated before, many higher order property tensors reduce to a scalar in high symmetry materials; those properties are not intrinsically scalar, but become scalar (i.e., isotropic) due to the crystallographic symmetry. Despite having only one value, those tensors should still be considered as higher rank tensors, meaning that they retain their subscripts, regardless of the crystal symmetry.

\subsubsection{Property Tensors of Rank 1 (Vectors)}

When the applied field is a vector and the response a scalar quantity, or the other way around, the proportionality factor must itself be a vector or a rank-1 tensor. The most important rank-1 material property tensors are shown in Table~\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensors1}; the table lists the property name, the symbol and SI units, the Field and Response quantities and finally whether this is an equilibrium (E) or transport (T) property. The final number shows the maximum number of independent components of the property tensor.

It is not uncommon in the literature to find alternative definitions of these quantities. For instance, the \indexit{electrocaloric vector} can also be interpreted as the proportionality factor between an applied electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and the resulting temperature change $\Delta T$. This relation can be obtained by combining eq.~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:specificheat}) with the relation $\Delta S=p_i\,E_i=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}$, resulting in:
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
The quantity in square brackets is also known as the electrocaloric vector, and it has SI units of $[m\,K/V]$. Similar alternative definitions can be found for the other rank-1 property tensors, so the reader should exercise caution when comparing different sources.

\subsubsection{Property Tensors of Rank 2}

There are many material properties that are represented by rank-2 tensors; some of the more important ones are listed in Table~\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensors2}.  For the SI units, the symbols F, H, and S refer to Farad, Henry, and Siemens, respectively;  $l_i$ are the direction cosines of the wave direction in a crystal, $G$ is the (dimensionless) \indexit{optical gyration constant}, and $\rho^a_i$ is the anti-symmetric part of the resistivity tensor (which can be written as a vector quantity, hence the single index).  As for the rank-1 tensor case, these relations can all be combined in many different ways, leading to additional material property descriptors.  The \indexit{thermoelectric power tensor} $\Sigma_{ij}$ is also known as the \indexit{Seebeck tensor}.

Rank-2 tensors with $6$ independent entries (labeled E6 in the last column of Table~\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensors2}) are symmetric tensors, i.e., $\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{ji}$. There are several potential reasons why a tensor is symmetric: in the case of the thermal expansion tensor, the symmetry of $\alpha_{ij}$  and $\alpha^{\prime}_{ij}$ is inherited from the symmetry of the strain and stress tensors, $\epsilon_{ij}$ and $\sigma_{ij}$. For the dielectric permittivity tensor $\kappa_{ij}$ and the magnetic permeability tensor $\mu_{ij}$, thermodynamic principles must be used to show that these tensors must be symmetric and we defer these arguments to section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:thermosymmetry}.  Similar thermodynamic arguments can be used to shows that several of the transport property tensors must be symmetric.

\subsubsection{Property Tensors of Rank higher than 4}

Property tensors of rank higher than $4$ in general represent non-linear effects; a few examples are listed in Table~\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensorshigher}. Note that in each case, the applied field is quadratic in either the electric field, or the stress/strain.  This leads to a large number of potential components, $3^5=243$ for the rank-5 tensors and $3^6=729$ for rank-6 tensors; the symmetry of the field and/or response quantities reduces those numbers, as shown in the last column of the table.  They are further reduced in the presence of crystallographic symmetry.  

In section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:matprop} we defined a general material property in terms of a Taylor expansion of the response $\mathcal{R}$ with respect to the applied field $\mathcal{F}$, and we introduced the linear approximation, i.e., the response is directly proportional to the field.  The higher rank tensors (rank $3$ and higher) come into play when we allow quadratic (and higher) field terms to contribute to the response.  As an illustration, let us consider strain to be the applied field and stress the response; the corresponding Taylor expansion up to terms quadratic in the field is then given by:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{ij} = c_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl} + c_{ijklmn}\epsilon_{kl}\epsilon_{mn}\ ,
\end{equation}
where the tensor components are defined in terms of partial derivatives of the stress with respect to the strain, evaluated at zero strain:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
	c_{ijkl} &= \left.\frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial\epsilon_{kl}}\right \vert_{\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{0}}\ ;\\ 
	c_{ijklmn} &= \frac{1}{2!}\left.\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{ij}}{\partial\epsilon_{kl}\partial\epsilon_{mn}}\right \vert_{\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{0}}\ . 	
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Similar expressions can be obtained for other non-linear (quadratic) property tensors, including electrostriction, the Kerr effect, and magnetoresistance (see Table~\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensors4}).  In the presence of multiple fields, the Taylor expansion will contain mixed partial derivatives.


\subsection{Engineering Notation for Tensors}\label{\chabbr:ssec:engnot}


% The attentive reader may have noticed that in the $K_{ij}$ matrix of eq.~\ref{PRPSYM:eq:Kmatrix} on page~\pageref{PRPSYM:eq:Kmatrix}, there are $36$ entries for the fourth rank elastic compliance tensor $s_{ijkl}$; they form a $6\times 6$ block along the diagonal of the matrix $K_{ij}$. But we know that a tensor of rank $n$ has $3^n$ components, which means that there are $3^4=81$ compliance components.  Similarly, for the stress and strain tensors, $\sigma_{ij}$ and $\epsilon_{ij}$, only six components are listed in the force and response vectors.  In section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:mechprop} we will see that both of these tensors are symmetric tensors, i.e., $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$ and $\epsilon_{ij}=\epsilon_{ji}$, so that effectively they have only six independent components, but it is unclear at this point why, for instance, the component $s_{zyzx}$ of the compliance tensor does not appear in the $K_{ij}$ matrix.




Since there are only $6$ independent components for both the stress and strain tensors, we may, instead of using $2$ indices that each run from $1$ to $3$, use a single index that runs from $1$ to $6$.  The only thing we need to do is define the correspondence between the double and single indices.  We define the \indexit{engineering notation} or \indexit{Voigt notation} by means of the following relation for the stress tensor:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\left(\begin{matrix}
	xx  &  xy  &  xz \\
	yx  &  yy  &  yz \\
	zx  &  zy  &  zz \end{matrix}\right)}
	\leftrightarrow
	\sigma^e_{\left(\begin{matrix}
	1  &  6  &  5 \\
	6  &  2  &  4 \\
	5  &  4  &  3 \end{matrix}\right)}\rightarrow \sigma^e_J = \left(\begin{matrix}
	\sigma_{1}\\ \sigma_{2}\\ \sigma_{3}\\ \sigma_{4}\\ \sigma_{5}\\ \sigma_{6}\end{matrix}\right) 
	= \left(\begin{matrix}
	\sigma_{xx}\\ \sigma_{yy}\\ \sigma_{zz}\\ \sigma_{yz}=\sigma_{zy}\\ \sigma_{xz}=\sigma_{zx}\\ \sigma_{xy}=\sigma_{yx}\end{matrix}\right). 
\end{equation}	
The numbers $1$ through $3$ along the diagonal represent the normal stresses, while the numbers $4$ to $6$ represent the shear stresses.  We will always denote the single index description with a superscript $^e$, to indicate that the engineering notation is being used, i.e., $\sigma_{xz} = \sigma^e_5$ and $\sigma_{yy} = \sigma^e_2\ldots$. Furthermore, we will use capital subscripts to indicate that the index goes from $1$ to $6$, e.g. $\sigma^e_J$.

The same indexing scheme can be applied to the strain tensor, with the exception that the engineering shear strains are equal to twice the original shear strains: 
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
	\epsilon^e_I&= \epsilon_{ii}\qquad\mbox{for $i=I=1,2,3$};\nonumber\\
	\epsilon^e_4 &= 2\epsilon_{yz};\\
	\epsilon^e_5 &= 2\epsilon_{xz};\\
	\epsilon^e_6 &= 2\epsilon_{xy}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The reason for the factor of two in the shear strain components is not difficult to understand.  We want the relation between strain and stress, in engineering notation, to read:
\begin{equation}
	\epsilon^e_I= s^e_{IJ} \sigma^e_J \qquad\left(= \sum_{J=1}^6 s^e_{IJ} \sigma^e_J\right).\label{\chabbr:eq:seIJ}
\end{equation}
Note that the compliances $s^e_{IJ}$ are represented by a $6\times 6$ matrix; intrinsic thermodynamic symmetry, which we will discuss in section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:thermosym}) requires that $s^e_{IJ}=s^e_{JI}$, in other words, the matrix $s^e_{IJ}$ must be a symmetric matrix.  Since $I$ and $J$ each stand for a pair of subscripts, we find that the compliance tensor itself must have the following symmetry:
\begin{equation}
	s_{ijkl} = s_{klij}.
\end{equation}
Furthermore, we know that both stress and strain tensors are symmetric, i.e., $\epsilon_{ij}=\epsilon_{ji}$ and $\sigma_{kl} = \sigma_{lk}$.  Hence we have:
\[
	s_{ijkl} = s_{jikl} = s_{ijlk} = s_{jilk}.
\]
These symmetries reduce the number of independent components from $81$ to $21$ which agrees with the number of independent components in the symmetric $s^e_{IJ}$ matrix, which is given by $(36-6)/2+6$.  

Using eq.~\ref{\chabbr:eq:seIJ}, we have in particular that:
\begin{eqnarray}
	\epsilon^e_1 &=& s^e_{11}\sigma^e_1+s^e_{12}\sigma^e_2+s^e_{13}\sigma^e_3+s^e_{14}\sigma^e_4+s^e_{15}\sigma^e_5+s^e_{16}\sigma^e_6;\label{\chabbr:eq:se1}\\
	\epsilon^e_4 &=& s^e_{41}\sigma^e_1+s^e_{42}\sigma^e_2+s^e_{43}\sigma^e_3+s^e_{44}\sigma^e_4+s^e_{45}\sigma^e_5+s^e_{46}\sigma^e_6\label{\chabbr:eq:se4}.
\end{eqnarray}
Starting with $\epsilon^e_1$, we also have (switching to numerical subscripts):
\begin{eqnarray*}
	\epsilon_{11} &=& s_{1111}\sigma_{11} + s_{1122}\sigma_{22} + s_{1133}\sigma_{33} +  s_{1112}\sigma_{12} +  s_{1121}\sigma_{21}  + \\ 
	&& s_{1113}\sigma_{13}+ s_{1131}\sigma_{31} + s_{1123}\sigma_{23} + s_{1132}\sigma_{32}. 
\end{eqnarray*}
Applying the symmetry of the compliance and stress tensors we can group a few terms:
\[	
	\epsilon_{11} = s_{1111}\sigma_{11} + s_{1122}\sigma_{22} + s_{1133}\sigma_{33} + 2 s_{1112}\sigma_{12} +2 s_{1113}\sigma_{13} +2 s_{1123}\sigma_{23}. 
\]
Since $\epsilon_{11}=\epsilon^e_1$, this expression must be equal to eq.~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:se1}); thus we must also have $2s_{1112} = s^e_{16}$, $2s_{1113}=s^e_{15}$ and $2s_{1123} = s^e_{14}$.

For $\epsilon^e_4$ we have two possibilities, since the Voigt index $4$ can stand for both index pairs $23$ and $32$;  we can deal with this by adding the expressions for $\epsilon_{23}$ and $\epsilon_{32}$ together:
\begin{eqnarray}
	\epsilon_{23} &=& s_{2311}\sigma_{11} + s_{2322}\sigma_{22} + s_{2333}\sigma_{33} + 2 s_{2312}\sigma_{12} +2 s_{2313}\sigma_{13} +2 s_{2323}\sigma_{23};\\
	\epsilon_{32} &=& s_{3211}\sigma_{11} + s_{3222}\sigma_{22} + s_{3233}\sigma_{33} + 2 s_{3212}\sigma_{12} +2 s_{3213}\sigma_{13} +2 s_{3223}\sigma_{23}.
\end{eqnarray}
The sum of these two equals (using the symmetry properties of the compliance tensor $s_{ijkl}$ and the strain tensor $\epsilon_{ij}$):
\[
	2\epsilon_{23} = 2s_{2311}\sigma_{11}+2s_{2322}\sigma_{22} + 2s_{2333}\sigma_{33} + 4 s_{2312}\sigma_{12} +4 s_{2313}\sigma_{13} +4 s_{2323}\sigma_{23}.
\]
Comparing this with the engineering notation in eq.~\ref{\chabbr:eq:se4}, we have $\epsilon_4^e=2\epsilon_{23}$, $s^e_{41}=2s_{2311}$, $s^e_{44}=4s_{2323}$ and so on.  Summarizing, we find the definition of the Voigt notation for the strain tensor shown earlier, as well as new relations for the compliance tensor:
\begin{subequations}\begin{align}
	s_{ijkl} &= s^e_{MN} \qquad\mbox{when $M$ and $N$ are $1,2$ or $3$};\\
	2s_{ijkl} &= s^e_{MN} \qquad\mbox{when either $M$ or $N$ is $4,5$ or $6$};\\
	4s_{ijkl} &= s^e_{MN} \qquad\mbox{when both $M$ and $N$ are $4,5$ or $6$}.
\end{align}\end{subequations}
These conversions allow for a single index notation to be used for stress and strain, and all material tensors related to them.  The disadvantage of using this notation is that the resulting quantities are no longer tensors and do not satisfy the tensor transformation equations.  To transform a material tensor from one reference frame to another requires the full tensor notation with all indices, not the engineering notation.  In other words, the relation
\[
	\epsilon_{ij} = s_{ijkl}\sigma_{kl}
\]
is a relation between tensors, but the relation
\[
	\epsilon^e_{I} = s^e_{IJ}\sigma^e_J
\]
is not.  However, as we will show in the following section, we can replace the usual $3\times 3$ transformation matrix $\alpha_{ij}$ by a $6\times 6$ matrix to perform the transformation using Voigt notation.

The same reasoning can be used to derive the engineering notation expressions for the other material property tensors in the $K_{ij}$ matrix that relate a response to the stress tensor; examples include thermal expansion and the piezoelectric effect:
\begin{subequations}\begin{align}
	\alpha_{ij} &= \alpha^e_K \qquad\mbox{when $K$ is $1,2$ or $3$};\\
	2\alpha_{ij} &= \alpha^e_K \qquad\mbox{when $K$ is $4,5$ or $6$};\\
	d_{ijk} &= d^e_{iL} \qquad\mbox{when $L$ is $1,2$ or $3$};\\
	2d_{ijk} &= d^e_{iL}\qquad\mbox{when $L$ is $4,5$ or $6$}.
\end{align}\end{subequations}


\subsection{Coordinate Transformations using Voigt notation}\label{\chabbr:sec:Voigttrafos}

Despite the fact that the engineering notation results in relations between quantities that are not tensors, it is possible to write down the transformation rules directly in engineering notation and obtain expressions that are similar to the regular tensor transformations. The relevant transformation matrix, $a_{IJ}$, is a $6\times 6$ matrix and its components can be derived quite easily by applying the standard tensor transformation to a symmetric rank-2 tensor, say $t_{ij}$, and then replacing the subscripts by engineering subscripts to obtain $t^e_I$.  We start from the relation:
\[
	t'_{ij} = \alpha_{ik}\alpha_{jl}t_{kl}
\]
and explicitly work out the matrix multiplication for a general transformation matrix $\alpha_{ij}$. For the transformed component $t'_{11}$ we obtain:
\[
	t'_{11} = \alpha^2_{11}t_{11}+\alpha^2_{12}t_{22}+\alpha^2_{13}t_{33}+2\alpha_{12}\alpha_{13}t_{23}+2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{13}t_{13}+2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{12}t_{12}\ .
\]
Switching to engineering notation, we obtain:
\[
	t^{e\prime}_{1} = \alpha^2_{11}t^e_{1}+\alpha^2_{12}t^e_{2}+\alpha^2_{13}t^e_{3}+2\alpha_{12}\alpha_{13}t^e_{4}+2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{13}t^e_{5}+2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{12}t^e_{6}\ .
\]
This means that the first row of the matrix $a_{IJ}$ contains the following terms:
\[
	a_{1J} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^2_{11}&\alpha^2_{12}&\alpha^2_{13}&2\alpha_{12}\alpha_{13}&2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{13}&2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{12}\end{pmatrix}\ .
\]
A similar derivation for the other components of $t^{e\prime}_{I}$ results in the following expression  for the full transformation matrix $a_{IJ}$:
{\small\begin{equation}
a_{IJ} = \left(\begin{array}{cccccc}
		\alpha^2_{11}&\alpha^2_{12}&\alpha^2_{13} &2\alpha_{12}\alpha_{13} &2\alpha_{13}\alpha_{11} &2\alpha_{11}\alpha_{12} \\
		\alpha^2_{21}&\alpha^2_{22}&\alpha^2_{23} &2\alpha_{22}\alpha_{23} &2\alpha_{23}\alpha_{21} &2\alpha_{21}\alpha_{22} \\
		\alpha^2_{31}&\alpha^2_{32}&\alpha^2_{33} &2\alpha_{32}\alpha_{33} &2\alpha_{33}\alpha_{31} &2\alpha_{31}\alpha_{32} \\
		\alpha_{21}\alpha_{31}&\alpha_{22}\alpha_{32} &\alpha_{23}\alpha_{33} &\alpha_{22}\alpha_{33}+\alpha_{23}\alpha_{32} &\alpha_{21}\alpha_{33}+\alpha_{23}\alpha_{31} &\alpha_{22}\alpha_{31}+\alpha_{21}\alpha_{32} \\
		\alpha_{31}\alpha_{11}&\alpha_{32}\alpha_{12} &\alpha_{33}\alpha_{13} &\alpha_{12}\alpha_{33}+\alpha_{13}\alpha_{32} &\alpha_{13}\alpha_{31}+\alpha_{11}\alpha_{33} &\alpha_{11}\alpha_{32}+\alpha_{12}\alpha_{31} \\
		\alpha_{11}\alpha_{21}&\alpha_{12}\alpha_{22} &\alpha_{13}\alpha_{23} &\alpha_{12}\alpha_{23}+\alpha_{13}\alpha_{22} &\alpha_{13}\alpha_{21}+\alpha_{11}\alpha_{23} &\alpha_{11}\alpha_{22}+\alpha_{12}\alpha_{21} \\
		\end{array}\right)\label{\chabbr:eq:aIJ}
\end{equation}}
This matrix is sometimes referred to as the \indexit{quadratic transformation matrix}.  The transformation rule for this rank-2 tensor then becomes:
\[
	t^{e\prime}_I = a_{IJ}t^e_J\qquad\text{($J$ runs from $1$ to $6$)}\ .
\]
As a second example we consider the elastic compliance tensor in Voigt notation, $s^e_{IJ}$.  If we transform the original tensor $s_{ijkl}$ using the standard relation for a rotation matrix $\alpha_{ij}$ we have:
\[
	s'_{ijkl} = \alpha_{io}\alpha_{jp}\alpha_{kq}\alpha_{lr} s_{opqr}\ .
\]
We would like for the transformation of the engineering matrix $s^e_{IJ}$ to have a similar mathematical form:
\[
	s^{\prime e}_{IJ} = a_{IK}s^e_{KL}a^{-1}_{JL} ,\qquad\text{(all subscripts run from $1$ to $6$)}\ ;
\]
it is not difficult, but somewhat tedious, to show that the matrix $a_{IJ}$ in equation~(\ref{\chabbr:eq:aIJ}) is also valid in this case.

The transformation of a rank-3 tensor that is symmetric in two of its indices represents an interesting case where both the $\alpha_{ij}$ and $a_{IJ}$ transformation matrices must be used.  Consider the piezoelectric effect, represented by the rank-3 tensor $d_{ijk}$; this tensor represents the proportionality between an applied stress $\sigma_{jk}$ and the resulting electric displacement $D_i$:
\[
	D_i = d_{ijk}\sigma_{jk}\ .
\]
Because the stress tensor is a symmetric tensor we must also have $d_{ijk}=d_{ikj}$, so that there are $18$ independent components instead of the usual $27$ for a rank-3 tensor.  We know that the vector $\mathbf{D}$ transforms using the $\alpha_{ij}$ matrix, and the symmetric stress tensor in engineering notation by means of the $a_{IJ}$ matrix, so that (omitting the subscripts and the superscript $^e$):
\[
	D' = \alpha\, D\text{ and } \bm{\sigma}^{\prime} = a\,\bm{\sigma}\, 
\]
In the primed reference frame we must have that $D' = d'\,\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}$, so that:
\[
	D' = \alpha\,D = \alpha\, d\,\bm{\sigma} = \alpha\, d\, a^{-1}\bm{\sigma}'\ .
\]
The result is the (mixed) transformation rule for the piezoelectric tensor:
\[
	d'_{iK} = \alpha_{ik}\,d_{kJ}\,(a^{-1})_{JK}\ .
\]
Note that the summations cover the range $1\ldots 3$ for $k$ and $1\ldots 6$ for $J$.


\section{Intrinsic Thermodynamic Symmetry}\label{\chabbr:sec:thermosym}
In this section, we take a look at material properties from the point of view of thermodynamics.  In thermodynamics, one represents the change in \textit{internal energy per unit volume},\index{internal energy} $\mathcal{U}$, as:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}\mathcal{U} = -P\mathrm{d}V+T\mathrm{d}S\ ,
\end{equation}
with $P$ = pressure,\index{pressure} $V$ = volume,\index{volume} $T$ = absolute temperature, and $S$ = entropy.\index{entropy}  The pairs $(P,V)$ and  $(T,S)$ are so-called \textit{conjugate pairs},\index{conjkugate pairs} and their product has the dimensions of an energy density. We can regard $(P,T)$ as ``forces'' and $(V,S)$ as ``responses.''  When the pressure increases, the system responds with a volume change, and when the temperature changes, the system adjusts its entropy.  In more general terms, the internal energy consists of two contributions, the heat absorbed by the system, $\mathrm{d}Q$,  and the work done by the system, $\mathrm{d}W$, so that
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}\mathcal{U} = \mathrm{d}Q-\mathrm{d}W\ .
\end{equation}
Work done \textit{by} the system has a \textit{positive} sign, whereas work done \textit{on} the system is \textit{negative}.

Pressure and temperature are not the only ``forces'' that we can define for a material system.  We can apply an external electric or magnetic field, we can exert a stress onto the system, and so on.  For each of those external fields, we can define an additional work term.  As described in detail in the example below, the magnetic field $H$ and the magnetic induction $B$ form a conjugate pair, with $H$ the ``force'' and $B$ the response.

\begin{example}
Let us consider a simple example: the work done by a magnetic solenoid.  A solenoid is essentially a wire which is wound in $N$ circular turns, so that the total length of the solenoid equals $L$.   The number of turns per unit length is defined as $n=N/L$.  If the solenoid is placed in a magnetic field which varies in time, then, according to electrodynamics, there will be a voltage induced across the two ends of the wire.  If we denote this electric voltage by $V_e$, to distinguish it from the volume $V$, then Faraday's \textit{Law of Induction}\index{Farady Induction Law} states that the voltage is equal to:
\begin{equation}
	V_e = N\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}\ ,
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the magnetic flux,\index{magnetic flux} i.e, the number of magnetic field lines through the cross sectional area of the solenoid: $\Phi = BA$, with $A$ the area and $B$ the magnetic induction.\index{magnetic induction}  The voltage can then be rewritten as:
\begin{equation}
	V_e = n V \frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}\ .
\end{equation}
The energy associated with this system can be written as $\mathrm{d}E=V_e\mathrm{d}q$, where $q$ is the electrical charge. It is easy to see that this expression has the dimensions of energy, since $V_e$ is measured in Volts, and $q$ in  Coulomb: $[VC = VAs = Ws = J]$.  Note that $q$ and $V_e$ are not conjugate variables, since their product does not have the dimensions of an energy density.  Introducing the electrical current $I=\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}t$, we find
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}E = V_e\mathrm{d}q = nV\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t} I\mathrm{d}t = nVI\mathrm{d}B\ .
\end{equation}
Since the magnetic field, $H$, produced by a solenoid is equal to $4\pi nI$, we have
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}E = \frac{V}{4\pi} H\mathrm{d}B\ .
\end{equation}
This can be converted to an energy density by dividing by the volume $V$:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}W = \frac{H\mathrm{d}B}{4\pi}\ ;
\end{equation}
we conclude that $(H,B)$ is a pair of conjugate variables, with $H$ the ``force'' and $B$ the ``response.''
\end{example}

In a similar way, it can be shown that the pair $(E,P)$, with $E$ the electric field,\index{electric field} and $P$ the polarization,\index{polarization} form a  conjugate pair with associated work term:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}W = \frac{E\mathrm{d}P}{4\pi}\ .
\end{equation}
Mechanical stress,\index{stress} $\sigma$, and strain,\index{strain} $\epsilon$, also form a conjugate pair with work term:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}W = \frac{1}{2}\sigma\mathrm{d}\epsilon\ ;
\end{equation}
the dimensions of stress are Pa(scal), and strain is dimensionless.  It is easy to see that Pa is an energy density: Pa = N/m$^2$ = Nm/m$^3$ = J/m$^3$.  The stress is a ``force'', and strain is the system's ``response.''

Let us now consider a general force $\mathcal{F}_j$ and its conjugate response $\mathcal{R}_i$.  According to the Taylor expansion derivation in section~\ref{\chabbr:sec:matprop}, there must be linear relations between forces and responses, so that we can define a general property, $K_{ij}$, for which
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{R}_i = K_{ij}\mathcal{F}_j\ .
\end{equation}
First of all, we can say something about the symmetry of $K_{ij}$.  We know that $\mathcal{U}$ is a \indexit{state function} of the forces $\mathcal{F}_i$.  That means that the following function (known as the \indexit{Helmholtz free energy}), 
\begin{equation}
	\Phi = \mathcal{U} - \mathcal{R}_i\mathcal{F}_i\ ,
\end{equation}
(summation over $i$) is also a state function.  We can then write, using the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}\Phi = \mathrm{d}\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{F}_i\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}_i-\mathcal{R}_i\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}_i\ .
\end{equation}
Using $\mathrm{d}\mathcal{U} = -P\mathrm{d}V+T\mathrm{d}S+\mathcal{F}_i\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}_i$, we find
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{d}\Phi = -P\mathrm{d}V+T\mathrm{d}S-\mathcal{R}_i\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}_i\ .
\end{equation}
Since $\Phi$ is a state function, $\mathrm{d}\Phi$ is a total (or exact) differential,\index{total differential} and hence:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \mathcal{R}_i}{\partial\mathcal{F}_j} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{R}_j}{\partial\mathcal{F}_i}\ .
\end{equation}
We also have $\mathcal{R}_i = K_{ij}\mathcal{F}_j$, so that we find $K_{ij} = K_{ji}$, i.e., the material property matrix $K_{ij}$ is a symmetric matrix.  This symmetry follows from thermodynamical considerations only and is hence an \indexit{intrinsic symmetry}.  It must be valid for all materials.

\subsection{Consequences of intrinsic symmetry}
Let us now consider the following forces $(\Delta T,\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H},\sigma_{ij})$. We can combine them into a single force vector with $13$ components:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{F}_j = (\Delta T, E_x, E_y, E_z, H_x, H_y, H_z, \sigma_{xx},\sigma_{yy},\sigma_{zz},\sigma_{xy},\sigma_{xz},
	\sigma_{yz})\ .
\end{equation}
Similarly, we can write for the components of the conjugate responses $(\Delta S,\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B},\epsilon_{ij})$:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{R}_i = (\Delta S,D_x,D_y,D_z,B_x,B_y,B_z,\epsilon_{xx},\epsilon_{yy},\epsilon_{zz},\epsilon_{xy},\epsilon_{xz},
	\epsilon_{yz})\ .

When we take a closer look at this large property matrix, we see that individual material properties show up as blocks within the larger matrix.  For instance, the dielectric permittivity tensor, $\kappa_{ij}$, occupies a $3\times 3$ block inside $K_{ij}$.  Since the large $K_{ij}$ matrix must be symmetric, this imposes restrictions on the component matrices. Along the diagonal of $K_{ij}$ we have the \textit{principal effects},\index{principal effect} which relate a force to its conjugate response.  All those material tensors must be symmetric.  Away from the diagonal we relate a force to the other (non-conjugate) responses (\textit{cross-effects}),\index{cross effects} and there are pair-wise relations between the material tensors of seemingly unrelated force-response pairs.  The entire matrix relation can be expressed more compactly in the following form:
\begin{align}
	\Delta S &= \frac{C}{T}\Delta T +p_iE_i + q_iH_i + \alpha'_{ij}\sigma_{ij}\ ;\\
	D_i &= p'_i\Delta T + \kappa_{ij}E_j + \lambda_{ij} H_j + d_{ijk}\sigma_{jk}\ ;\\
	B_i &= q'_i\Delta T + \lambda'_{ij}E_j + \mu_{ij} H_j + Q_{ijk}\sigma_{jk}\ ;\\
	\epsilon_{ij} &= \alpha_{ij}\Delta T +d'_{ijk}E_k + Q'_{ijk} H_k + s_{ijkl}\sigma_{kl}\ .
\end{align}
We make in particular the following observations:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The material tensors along the diagonal, $\kappa_{ij}$, $\mu_{ij}$, and $s_{ijkl}$ are symmetric tensors because $K_{ij}$ is symmetric.  This means that $\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{ji}$, $\mu_{ij}=\mu_{ji}$, and also $s_{ijkl} = s_{klij}$.  These are intrinsic (thermodynamic) symmetries for the principal property tensors.
	
	\item The material tensors away from the diagonal are related to each other in the following way:  we have $D_i = \ldots + d_{ijk}\sigma_{jk}$ and $\epsilon_{jk} = \ldots +d'_{jki}E_i+\ldots$.  Since the matrix $K_{ij}$ is symmetric, we must also have $d_{ijk} = d'_{jki}$, or, in words, the components of the piezoelectric tensor $d_{ijk}$ are identical to the components of the converse piezoelectric tensor $d'_{jki}$ (although the units used for each are different).  Note the different order of the indices!  Similar relations can be found for $Q'_{pqr} = Q_{rpq}$ (piezomagnetic and converse piezomagnetic tensors), and $\lambda'_{ij} = \lambda_{ji}$ (direct and converse magnetoelectric polarizabilities).  
	
	\item For the entries on the first row or column, we have $\alpha'_{ij} = \alpha_{ij}$, and since there are only $6$ components for this $3\times 3$ matrix, it must be symmetric, i.e., $\alpha_{ij}=\alpha_{ji}$.  This means that the thermal expansion coefficients and the piezocaloric coefficients are identical and symmetric.  The same can be said for the electrocaloric and pyroelectric coefficients, $p'_i = p_i$, and the magnetocaloric and pyromagnetic coefficients, $q'_i=q_i$.  
\end{itemize}
The nature of the thermodynamic energy functions, in particular the fact that they are state functions, has far-reaching consequences for the intrinsic symmetry of material properties. Thermodynamic path independence requires that the dielectric permittivity tensor $\kappa_{ij}$ (often also denoted as $\epsilon_{ij}$) be a symmetric tensor.  The same is true for the magnetic permeability and the elastic compliances.  This symmetry requirement is a nice illustration of the importance of symmetry in the context of material properties.  In the following section, we will consider the effect of crystallographic symmetry on the material property tensors that make up the larger $K_{ij}$ matrix.


\section{Crystallographic Symmetry and the Equilibrium Property Matrix $K_{ij}$}\label{\chabbr:sec:CrySymK}

In this section, we will analyze how crystal symmetry affects the overall property matrix $K_{ij}$. We have already seen in section~\ref{\chabbr:ssec:propsym} how we can apply symmetry operators to tensors: we simply use the standard transformation rule for the tensor and replace the matrix $\alpha_{ij}$ by the relevant symmetry operator matrices. The example in section~\ref{\chabbr:ssec:propsym} used the symmetric magnetic permeability tensor, $\mu_{ij}$, which is a principal effect, to illustrate the process of determining the independent tensor components.  For the off-diagonal or cross-effect material tensors in $K_{ij}$, such as the magnetoelectric polarizability tensor $\lambda_{ij}$, which is \textit{not} symmetric, we find (exercise) using the same procedure for point group \pg{9}:
\begin{equation}
	\lambda_{ij} =  \left[\begin{array}{ccc} 
	\lambda_{xx} & \lambda_{xy} & 0 \\ 
	-\lambda_{xy} &  \lambda_{xx} & 0 \\ 
	0 & 0 & \lambda_{zz}\end{array}\right].
\end{equation}

This procedure can be repeated for all material tensors of all ranks.  For ranks higher than 2, the procedure becomes somewhat tedious, because the number of matrix products to work out increases rapidly with increasing tensor rank, but it is not very difficult. Group theory offers several very powerful tools to simplify this task, in particular group representation theory and group character tables, but this approach is a bit beyond the level of the present chapter. If we repeat the procedure above for all material tensors that make up the matrix $K_{ij}$ of the previous section, then we obtain the effective property matrix for a crystal with point group \pg{9}:

	\left(\begin{array}{c|ccc|ccc|cccccc}

For point group \pg{9}, the number of independent components is further reduced to $91-62=29$.  In other words, to fully describe the response of a material with point symmetry \pg{9} to an external set of forces consisting of $(\Delta T, \mathbf{E}, \mathbf{H}, \sigma_{ij})$, we need to know $29$ independent constants, which are grouped in various material tensors. Note that for the cubic symmetry, all material tensors of rank less than $4$ either vanish completely or are equal to a scalar; only the elastic compliance tensor $s_{ijkl}$, of rank $4$, does not reduce to a scalar.  We learn from this matrix that cubic materials can not exhibit the following equilibrium properties: electrocaloric, magnetocaloric, pyroelectric, pyromagnetic, direct and converse piezoelectricity and piezomagnetism; crystallographic symmetry simply does not allow them. It is also clear that the principal effects can never vanish; all materials have a specific heat constant, a dielectric tensor, a magnetic permeability tensor, and a compliance tensor.  

It is important to realize that the form of $K_{ij}$ for a given crystallographic symmetry directly determines how many property measurements need to be carried out to fully determine the relations between the fields $[\Delta T, \mathbf{E}, \mathbf{H}, \bm{\sigma}]$ and the corresponding responses $[\Delta S, \mathbf{D}, \mathbf{B}, \bm{\epsilon}]$. Only $8$ measurements need to be carried out for symmetry \pg{32}: specific heat, thermal expansion, dielectric constant, magnetic permeability, magnetoelectric polarizability (all of which are scalars), and three elastic compliances. \begin{align}
	\Delta S &= \frac{C}{T}\Delta T + \alpha \text{Tr}(\bm{\sigma})\ ;\\
	D_i &=  \kappa E_i + \lambda H_i\ ;\\
	B_i &=  \lambda E_i + \mu H_i\ ;\\
	\epsilon_{ij} &= \alpha\Delta T\delta_{ij} + s_{ijkl}\sigma_{kl}\ .
\end{align}
This symmetry analysis thus directly tells us which measurements to carry out.

\section{Crystallographic Symmetry and the Transport Property Matrix $L_{ij}$}\label{\chabbr:sec:CrySymL}

When we discussed equilibrium properties and thermodynamic symmetry earlier in this chapter, we made use of the path independence of thermodynamic state functions to show that the property matrix $K_{ij}$ must have intrinsic symmetry.  Then we applied crystallographic symmetry to further reduce the number of independent material tensor components.  In the present section we will do the same for transport properties, i.e., those properties that have to do with the motion of electrical charges, vacancies and atoms, and heat.  We will find that many of the principles used for the equilibrium properties remain valid, but there are subtle differences, in particular in the presence of magnetic fields.  

In what follows, we will assume that the system is only slightly out of equilibrium; this will allow us to apply the \indexit{principle of microscopic reversibility} and Onsager's \indexit{reciprocity theorem}. This principle states that as long as the particles move over short distances in response to a gradient, a reversal of the sign of the gradient would make all particles reversibly return to their original positions; in other words, over short distances, i.e., microscopic distances, the system is reversible. Moreover, the system response in steady-state conditions, generally known as a \indexit{flux}, is considered to be linear in the gradients, which take the place of the forces from the equilibrium properties.  In other words, for small deviations from equilibrium and in steady-state, the terms (Force, Response) used for equilibrium properties are replaced by (Gradient, Flux) for transport properties; to first order, the relations between these two are once again linear, and we expect to find both principal and cross effects, as was the case for equilibrium properties.

\subsection{Transport Properties and Thermodynamic Symmetry}\label{\chabbr:ssec:Lintrinsicsymmetry}

We will assume that there is a small departure from equilibrium, and also that the material is in a steady-state condition, meaning that the departure from equilibrium does not depend on time.  A departure from equilibrium can in general be described by a gradient operation. For instance, when a concentration gradient is created from one side of a material to the other, there will be a flow of mass in response to the gradient.  When a gradient of electrical potential (i.e., an electric field) is applied, then there will be a flow of electrical charges, and so on.  In Tables~\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensors1}--\ref{\chabbr:tb:tensors4}, we introduced a number of gradients:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Temperature gradient: $\bm{\nabla} T$ is the temperature gradient at a given point in a material.  It is a vector quantity (tensor of rank 1), defined by:
		\[
		\bm{\nabla} T = \frac{\partial T}{\partial x_1}\mathbf{e}_1+
		\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_2}\mathbf{e}_2+
		\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_3}\mathbf{e}_3 = \nabla_j T\mathbf{e}_j\ ,
		\]
		where $T=T(\mathbf{r})$ is the temperature field, and $\nabla_j=\partial/\partial x_j$.  The material response to this gradient is a \indexit{heat flow}, represented by a vector $\mathbf{h}$.  The relation between heat flow and temperature gradient is given by indexit{Fourier's Law of heat conduction}:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{h} = -\bm{K}\bm{\nabla} T\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:FouriersLaw}
	\end{equation}
	or, in component notation:
	\[
		h_i = -K_{ij}\nabla_j T\ .
	\]
	The gradient can be regarded as a ``force,'' and the heat flow as the ``response'' of the material.  The minus sign indicates that heat flows downhill (the gradient operator always produces a vector that points uphill).  The tensor $\bm{K}$ is known as the \indexit{thermal conductivity tensor}.  If the temperature gradient is measured in units of $[K/m]$, and the heat flow as $[J/m^2/s]$, then the thermal conductivity is measured in units of $[W/m/K]$ (Watts per meter per Kelvin).
		
	\item Electrical potential gradient: $\bm{\nabla} \phi_e$ is the electrical potential gradient at a point in the material.  It is a vector quantity (tensor of rank 1), and is usually written as the electric field vector 
	\[
		\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = -\bm{\nabla} \phi_e(\mathbf{r})\ .
	\]
	The material response to this gradient is an \indexit{electrical current flow}, represented by a vector $\mathbf{j}$.  The relation between current flow and potential gradient is given by \indexit{Ohm's Law}:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{j} = -\bm{\sigma}\bm{\nabla} \phi_e = \bm{\sigma}\mathbf{E}\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:OhmsLaw}
	\end{equation}
	or, in component notation:
	\[
		j_i = \sigma_{ij}E_j=-\sigma_{ij}\nabla_j\phi_e\ .
	\]
	The tensor $\bm{\sigma}$ is known as the \indexit{electrical conductivity} tensor.  If the potential gradient is measured in units of $[V/m]$, and the current flow as $[C/m^2/s]$, then the electrical conductivity is measured in units of $[1/m \Omega]$ (per meter per Ohm, $\Omega = V/A$).
	
	\item Concentration gradient: $\bm{\nabla} c$ is the concentration gradient at a point in the material.  It is a vector quantity (tensor of rank 1). The material response to this gradient is a \indexit{mass flux}, represented by a vector $\mathbf{m}$.  The relation between mass flow and concentration gradient is given by 
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{m} = -\bm{D}\bm{\nabla} c\ ,\label{\chabbr:eq:FicksFirstLaw}
	\end{equation}
	(known as \indexit{Fick's first law}) or, in component notation:
	\[
		m_i = -D_{ij}\nabla_j c\ .
	\]
	The tensor $\bm{D}$ is known as the \indexit{diffusivity} tensor.  If the concentration gradient is measured in units of $[atoms/m^3/m]$, and the mass flow as $[atoms/m^2/s]$, then the diffusivity is measured in units of $[m^2/s]$ (meter squared per second).
\end{itemize}

It, too, can be considered as the gradient of a potential, in this case the (scalar) magnetic potential $\phi_m$.\footnote{We call it a scalar potential, to distinguish it from the magnetic vector potential, which can also be used to describe the magnetic induction in the presence of currents.} We will return to magnetic fields later on, because their effect is significantly more complex than that of the other three gradients we have discussed so far.

Note that the three material tensors that we have introduced, $\bm{K}, \bm{\sigma}$, and $\bm{D}$, relate a gradient of a particular type to the corresponding flux \indexit{of the same type} (i.e, thermal gradient to thermal flux, electrical gradient to electrical flux, ...).  This means that those tensors represent \indexit{principal effects}. We should also allow for \indexit{cross effects} to occur, for instance, an electrical current induced by a temperature gradient. For equilibrium properties, we required that the product of a force and its conjugate response have the dimensions of energy density.  For transport properties, one can show that this product must be equal to $T\dot{S}$, with $\dot{S}=\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t$, the rate of entropy density production in the system in units of J/sKm$^3$.  It is easy to verify, for instance, that the product of $\mathbf{m}$ and $\bm{\nabla} c$ does not have the required dimensions which indicates that the gradients as listed above are not the actual thermodynamic forces that are needed to obtain conjugate pairs with the correct dimensions.

Let us denote a flux by the general symbol $J$, and the conjugate thermodynamic force by the symbol $X$.  If the departure from equilibrium is small, we can once again write down a linear relation between forces and fluxes of the form:
\[
	J_{i}  = L_{ij} X_j,
\]
where $L_{ij}$ is written instead of $K_{ij}$, to distinguish between equilibrium and transport properties.  The arguments of Onsager's theory are both subtle and complex and belong to the domain of irreversible thermodynamics. For our purposes, it will be sufficient to state that, if the correct thermodynamic forces $X_j$ and fluxes $J_i$ are used, then the matrix $L_{ij}$ must be symmetric,  i.e., $L_{ij}=L_{ji}$; in particular,  this means that the principal transport property tensors, $\bm{K}, \bm{\sigma}$, and $\bm{D}$, must be symmetric tensors.

\subsection{Principal and cross transport effects}\label{\chabbr:ssec:Lprincipaleffects}

As an example of the application of Onsager's reciprocity theorem, we consider here the combination of thermal and electrical transport properties. The correct thermodynamic forces $\bm{X}$ can be shown to be given by:
\begin{align}
	\bm{X}^t &= -\frac{1}{T}\bm{\nabla}T;\\
	\bm{X}^e &= -\bm{\nabla}\bar{\phi},
\end{align}
where $\bar{\phi} = \phi_e - \mu/e$ with $\mu$ the electrochemical potential and $\phi_e$ the electrical potential. The resulting flux equations are then written as
\begin{align}
	j_i &= \sigma_{ij} X^e_j - \beta_{ij} X^t_j;\\
	h_i &= -\beta'_{ij}X^e_j +\gamma_{ij} X^t_j.
\end{align}
The $L_{ij}$ matrix is thus given by:
\begin{equation}
	L = \left(\begin{array}{cc} \bm{\sigma} & -\bm{\beta}\\ 
	-\bm{\beta}' & \bm{\gamma}\end{array}\right).
\end{equation}
Application of $L_{ij}=L_{ji}$ indicates that $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$, $\gamma_{ij}=\gamma_{ji}$ and $\beta_{ij} = \beta'_{ji}$; in other words, $\bm{\sigma}$ and $\bm{\gamma}$ must be symmetric tensors, and $\bm{\beta}$ and $\bm{\beta}'$ must be each other's transpose (but not necessarily symmetric).  In the absence of a thermal gradient, $\bm{\sigma}$ is the electrical conductivity tensor;  $\bm{\gamma}$ is related to the thermal conductivity tensor, as we will now show.

It is customary to rewrite the equations in terms of the current density $\mathbf{j}$ and the temperature gradient $\bm{\nabla}T$ as the independent variables:
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{E} &= \bm{\rho}\mathbf{j} - \bm{\Sigma}\bm{\nabla} T;\\
	\mathbf{h} &= -T\bm{\Sigma}'\mathbf{j} - \bm{K}\bm{\nabla} T.
\end{align}
In these equations, $\bm{\rho} = \bm{\sigma}^{-1}$ is the \indexit{resistivity tensor}. The tensor $\bm{\Sigma}$ is defined by:
\begin{equation}
	\bm{\Sigma} = \frac{\bm{\rho}\bm{\beta}}{T},
\end{equation}
and is known as the \indexit{thermoelectric power} tensor; $T\bm{\Sigma}'$ is often replaced by the \indexit{Peltier effect tensor}, $\bm{\pi}$.  We also have that $\bm{\Sigma}'$ is the transpose of $\bm{\Sigma}$, and for the thermal conductivity tensor we find:
\begin{equation}
	\bm{K} = \frac{1}{T}\left(\bm{\gamma} - \bm{\beta}'\bm{\rho}\,\bm{\beta}\right).
\end{equation}
It is thus clear that the Onsager reciprocity theorem results in relations between the various material transport property tensors.
